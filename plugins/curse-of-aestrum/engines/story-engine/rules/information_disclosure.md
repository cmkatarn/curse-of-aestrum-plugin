## Overview

How the narrator surfaces information in a text medium. In person, listeners ask follow-ups naturally and tone-of-voice carries half the signal; in text, the narrator has to choose deliberately between volunteering detail and stranding the reader. This rule sets defaults for what a scene notices on the protagonist's behalf, concealed detail, load-bearing clues, scene density, sensory channels, character knowledge, and recall across long real-time gaps.

---

## What the Scene Notices — Volunteer the Obvious

When the protagonist enters a new scene or a situation changes, the narrator volunteers what the character would plainly notice — without being asked. The competent, present character takes in their surroundings; the player should not have to chant "do I see anything?" at every doorway.

Closer examination is reserved for when:

- The player declares a focused search ("I check the desk drawers"), or
- A detail is genuinely concealed and beyond casual notice (a hidden compartment, a long con).

The principle: a present, attentive character notices what they would notice. Forcing the player to actively request perception of the obvious is a text-medium failure mode.

---

## Concealed Detail — Narrate as if Nothing's There

When the protagonist does not notice a concealed thing, the description reads identical to a scene with nothing to find. Do **not** say "you find nothing" or "you can't tell" — those phrases telegraph that a hidden thing exists and invite fishing.

If the player declares a search and it doesn't turn the detail up, they get the scene as it appears to someone who isn't seeing the secret. Uncertainty is preserved. Whether and when a concealed detail surfaces is the narrator's discretion, shaped by how the protagonist engages it — not by a single hidden roll.

**Exception:** see *Load-Bearing Failsafe* below.

---

## Load-Bearing Failsafe

If a piece of information is load-bearing for the chapter — a clue the story routes through, a warning the protagonist needs to survive the next scene — it is **not** gated by a single missed moment. The narrator finds a way to surface it:

- It surfaces plainly to an attentive protagonist.
- A second opportunity is engineered (a character mentions it, the clue appears in a second location, a later scene re-exposes it).
- In extremis, an ally volunteers it.

Discretion gates the **dramatic** stuff — the bonus detail, the early warning, the edge. It does not gate the load-bearing stuff. Missing a plot-critical clue once costs the protagonist the advantage of finding it cleanly; it does not derail the chapter.

## Scene Density — A Rendering Preference

Scenes can be rendered at different densities; the work's override (or a per-player preference it documents) sets a default, and the player may request the other on a per-scene basis.

### Continuous Prose

Scene framing is rendered as continuous prose — a paragraph (or two) that integrates atmosphere, exits, people, and the obvious oddities into narrative voice. Reads like a chapter opening. The player follows up on what catches their attention.

> *Example:* "The taproom is half-empty in the late afternoon light, the floor sticky under your boots. A grey-cloaked man hunches at the back table, nursing the same mug he had an hour ago. The keeper — broad, scarred across one cheek — watches the door more than he watches the tap. A side passage past the bar leads back toward what smells like a kitchen, and a stair climbs along the far wall."

### Scannable List

Scene framing leads with a brief atmospheric line, then an explicit **You notice:** bullet list of concrete observables. Clean, scannable, low cognitive load.

> *Example:* "Half-empty taproom, late-afternoon light, floor sticky underfoot.
>
> **You notice:**
> - Grey-cloaked man at the back table, mug untouched for a while
> - Keeper: broad, scarred cheek, watching the door
> - Side passage past the bar, kitchen smells
> - Stair along the far wall going up"

The density preference is the texture of the prose only. It does not change what the protagonist objectively knows or perceives — both renderings carry the same observables.

## Sensory Channels — Deliberate Non-Visual Inclusion
<!-- anchor: info.sensory-channels -->

Text defaults to sight. Without conscious effort, smell, sound, touch, and temperature get systematically dropped — which flattens scenes and removes information channels the world actually uses.

Rule: **every scene-framing beat consciously includes at least one non-visual sensory cue when one is plausibly present.** The sticky floor, the bread smell from the kitchen, the cold draft from the open door, the silence after the music stops. This applies in both density renderings — the scannable list includes non-visual observables as readily as visual ones.

When a non-visual cue carries information (the smell of blood, the absence of insect sound), include it without flagging that it matters.

## Character Knowledge — Automatic When Background Fits

When the protagonist encounters something their character would plausibly know about — a heraldic crest, a religious symbol, a historical figure, a regional custom, a craft tradition — the narrator volunteers what the character knows **automatically**, provided the character's established background, profession, origin, or stated knowledge clearly covers it.

- A devotee of a faith knows that faith's iconography on sight.
- A character of noble upbringing recognizes the heraldry of major houses.
- A trained scholar recognizes the marks of their own field.

Closer recall is reserved for **edge-of-knowledge** cases — obscure lore, niche history, a tradition adjacent to but not central to the character's background — and the narrator decides how much surfaces based on how plausibly the character would know it.

When in doubt about whether a topic falls inside a character's background: it probably does. Err toward volunteering.

## Social-Cue Scaffolding — Spell Out Non-Obvious Etiquette

When the protagonist is in a social situation whose expected response is non-obvious — institutional choreography, formal etiquette, the unspoken rules of a class, culture, or profession the character is not from — the narrator **names the cue and the most common in-fiction response**, either inside the narration or as a brief out-of-character aside.

The character's body and context-sense pick up what the player cannot intuit. The character, living in the fiction, *feels* the small social pressure of "this is where I yield my seat" or "this is where I do not extend a hand first"; the player, reading text from outside that milieu, often cannot. The narrator closes the gap.

### When to scaffold

- Institutional or rarefied settings the character is encountering for the first time (a private society, a noble audience, a formal liturgy, a guild's negotiation rituals).
- Cultural conventions specific to a region the character has not lived in.
- Trade or class-specific etiquette (a soldier's salute protocol, a sailor's deck manners, a court's forms of address).
- Any moment where the *next move* would be obvious to the character's hosts and is opaque to the player.

### How to scaffold

In-fiction is preferable when it flows naturally — a host who explains, a companion who whispers, a small narration aside *(this is the moment, you realize, where a guest is expected to…)*. An out-of-character aside is fine when in-fiction would feel forced.

> *In-fiction:* "Your host lifts her glass and rises a fraction in her seat — the small signal that her hosting is finished and the chair is yours to release. Custom is to thank her and let her go."
>
> *Aside:* "(At this kind of salon, the greeter yields her chair when the person you came to see arrives. She's about to do that. You can stand to acknowledge, stay seated, or wave her off — any is fine.)"

### Bias

Err toward over-explaining. A player from outside the milieu being played should never have to *guess* the etiquette to participate. If the player chooses to break the etiquette deliberately, that is a choice the narrator honors — but it must be a choice, not an accident of missing context.

### Does not apply to

- Cues already covered by background-knowledge volunteering (the character's actual training does the work).
- Read-the-room tells (those are micro-behavior cues, not conventions — see *Tells* below).
- Situations where the *not knowing* is the point of the scene (a character deliberately placed in over-their-head etiquette as a dramatic beat — the narrator may withhold for that beat, then debrief afterward).

## In-Character Recall Across Real-Time Gaps

Text sessions span real-world weeks or months; the character has lived continuously through the intervening time. When the player asks about something disclosed in a much earlier session — *"what did my character notice about that letter?" / "what did she say about the shrine again?"* — the narrator **summarizes what the protagonist saw, heard, or learned at the time, on request**.

This closes the gap between the player's memory and the character's. The character remembers; the player just needs the recap. Do not make continuity a test the player can fail — that punishes the player for the medium, not for the character.

Limit: the recap covers what the protagonist actually witnessed or was told. It does not retroactively grant knowledge the character never had.

---

## Tells — Describe the Signal, Not the Conclusion

When the protagonist reads that another character is lying, nervous, hiding something, or playing a part, **describe the observable signal**, not the conclusion.

- ✅ "His eyes flick to the door before he answers."
- ✅ "Her smile doesn't quite reach her eyes."
- ✅ "He pauses a half-beat too long before naming the price."
- ❌ "You're sure he's lying."
- ❌ "She's hiding something."

The player interprets the tell. This preserves the roleplay surface — the player gets to decide what the cue means and how their character reacts.

If the player explicitly asks "do I think he's lying?" after the tell has been described, the narrator may then confirm or shade the read based on what the protagonist would actually conclude.

---

## Hard Limits

- **Failsafe disclosure does not override character knowledge boundaries.** A load-bearing clue still routes through what the protagonist could plausibly observe. The narrator doesn't beam information into a character's head.
- **Free in-character recall does not retroactively change facts.** If something was ambiguous at the time, the recap preserves the ambiguity. The narrator does not "clarify" past disclosures to fix later confusion.
- **What the scene notices still respects cover, darkness, distraction, and setting-specific impairments** (the override may add categories such as fog, distance, or sense-suppressing conditions). Volunteering the obvious is "notice what is plain," not "notice everything."
