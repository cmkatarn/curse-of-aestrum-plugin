# Personality — Authoring Rule

Authoring guidance for the **personality layer** of a character — the part of a
sheet that decides how they behave. This file is about how to *design* one so it
holds up in play; how a consuming work names its fields is that work's business.

The principle: **a personality is a generator, not a description.** A sheet's job
is not to summarize how a character has behaved. It is to hand the narrator
enough machinery to produce the behavior *that character* would produce in a
situation the author never anticipated. Everything below is judged against that:
a line that only summarizes is decoration; a line that generates is the field
doing its work.

---

## The Adjective Trap

The commonest failure in personality authoring is writing traits as adjectives.

An adjective is a **compression of past behavior.** Handed one, a narrator
decompresses it to the population average — "blunt" produces generic bluntness,
"warm" produces generic warmth — and two characters who share an adjective become
the same person the moment they share a scene.

**Write the mechanism, not the label.** For every trait, the sheet answers one
question: *what rule is this character running that produces that behavior?*

- **Label:** *Alice is blunt.*
- **Mechanism:** *Alice has a different threshold for what counts as a private
  matter, asks the question in a tone of mild interest, and is genuinely
  unprepared for the reaction.*

The second is playable in a scene nobody authored. The first is not.

**The test:** if a line could be moved verbatim onto another character's sheet
without anything about it needing to change, it is a label, not a mechanism.
Rewrite it or cut it.

## The Four Generators

Four things generate behavior. A character missing any of them drifts toward the
narrator's defaults in play, and drifts fastest under pressure — which is exactly
where scenes go.

### 1. Wants

At two scales:

- **The standing want** — what they are organizing their life around right now.
- **The idle want** — what they reach for with a free evening and no obligations.

A character with no authored want is **reactive furniture.** In play they will
agree with whoever spoke last, because nothing in the sheet pulls the other way.
This is the most commonly omitted element in character authoring and the most
expensive omission.

A want is a **direction, not an itinerary.** *Bob wants to be taken seriously by
people who currently don't* is a want. *Bob will confront Carol on Thursday* is a
plan, and plans belong to play, not to the sheet.

### 2. The Guarded Thing

Wants pull; the guarded thing pushes. It is what they are organizing to avoid,
and it is usually mundane — a reputation, a routine, a self-image, one
relationship, a version of events they need to stay standing.

Want and guarded thing together generate most of a character's decisions with no
further authoring. If a scene ever asks *what would they do here?* and the sheet
is silent, these two are the fields that were missing.

**Keep it narrow — a broad guarded thing forecloses the character's own want.**
This is the commonest way the field goes wrong, and it fails silently. If what
they guard is a whole *category* of experience — closeness, seriousness,
conflict, obligation — and their want requires passing through that category,
the sheet has authored a **deadlock**, and in play the character simply never
moves. Worse, it reads as depth while it does it.

**State the specific thing they cannot risk, not the register they avoid.**
*Being seen to ask for more than their share* is narrow, mundane, and playable —
it bites on one request. *Letting anything get heavy* is a register, and it
silently cancels every want that needs another person's cooperation.

**The test — name the scene in which they get what they want.** Walk it through.
If the guarded thing fires in that scene and stops it before it starts, the
guarded thing is too broad. Narrow it until the want stays **reachable** and the
difficulty sits *inside* the moment rather than in front of it. This is the same
principle `rule_connectable_companions` applies to flaws — bite on the depth and
expression of the thing, never on its formation — and it applies to the guarded
thing with equal force.

### 3. The Contradiction

**The highest-yield line on any sheet, and the one that separates a character
from a type.** Two things that are both true of them and do not reconcile.

Generic shapes: the generous one who keeps score. The fearless one who will not
make a phone call. The person who says everything out loud and cannot say one
particular sentence. The one who needs to be looked at and cannot be seen.

Two requirements:

- **Both halves must be genuinely true.** A contradiction where one half is a
  performance is not a contradiction — it is a disguise, which is a different and
  lesser device.
- **It must be load-bearing** — visible in what they *do*, not only in what they
  feel. If the contradiction never changes an action, it is backstory.

A character with no contradiction is an adjective with a name.

### 4. Pressure Behavior

What they **specifically do** when cornered, embarrassed, caught out,
contradicted, or bored. Not "brave" or "anxious" — the action itself: deflects
with a joke, goes quiet and very precise, gets louder, asks a question back,
leaves the room, starts tidying something.

This is the **strongest anti-bleed device available**, because two characters
with identical temperament diverge instantly under pressure.

**Answer "bored" specifically.** Most sheets cover threat and none cover tedium,
and tedium is far commoner in play than danger. A character who has no authored
response to a dull evening will be written as patient by default, which is
frequently wrong.

## The Second Tier

Real, and worth authoring — but **fold these in as beats, not as their own
sections.** Every additional top-level field costs adherence across all the
others; a schema that names fifteen things gets fifteen things done badly.

- **Taste.** What they find funny, beautiful, contemptible, cheap, embarrassing.
  Badly underrated: taste drives hundreds of small in-scene choices — what they
  order, where they sit, how they judge a stranger's clothes — that a narrator
  otherwise improvises with nothing to ground it.
- **Physical grammar.** Behavior, not appearance. Where the hands go, what they
  do while listening, default posture, what they touch. This is what lets prose
  render them with no dialogue at all.
- **Audience calibration.** How they differ with strangers, with intimates, with
  someone they want something from, with someone below them in whatever hierarchy
  is operating. A personality identical in every room is a cartoon — **the gap
  between the public and the private version is the characterization.**
- **Operating history.** Not biography. *Bob's father was a carpenter* is
  biography; *Bob cannot leave a wobbling table alone, because his father was a
  carpenter* is operating history. Only the second changes a scene. If a past
  fact does not currently produce behavior, it belongs in the overview.
- **Self-concept against reality.** What they believe they are good at, and
  whether they are right. The gap is a permanent generator. This one is
  **author-facing** — put it wherever the consuming work keeps hidden material,
  never in character-facing prose.

## Voice, Decomposed

Voice is the one part of personality that **must not be blended into temperament
prose.** Blended, it gets swallowed by the surrounding description and stops
being enforceable. Give it its own field, and answer:

- **Lexicon** — words they reach for, and words they never use.
- **Syntax** — long or short, complete or fragmentary, subordinate clauses or
  none.
- **Tempo** — fast, measured, halting; whether they leave gaps.
- **Humor** — whether they are funny at all, and in what mode: dry, silly, cruel,
  punning, absent.
- **Address** — do they use names, titles, nicknames they invented themselves?
- **Silence** — what they do with it: fill it, use it, or fail to notice it.
- **Under stress** — *the one authors skip.* Does formality go **up** or **down**?
  Do they get shorter or longer? More profane or less? Does an accent, dialect,
  or first language surface or vanish? A single answer here does more for voice
  consistency than a paragraph of cadence description.

**The bleed test:** take three lines of this character's dialogue and put them in
another character's mouth. If nothing is lost, the voice field has not done its
job yet.

## The Floor

A **complete** character answers five things:

1. The standing want.
2. The guarded thing.
3. The contradiction.
4. What they do under pressure — including boredom.
5. What their voice does under stress.

Everything else in this rule is optional depth. A character who answers all five
can be played into an unanticipated scene; a character who answers none will be
improvised from the narrator's defaults no matter how long the sheet is.

For **stubs**, see `rule_progressive_authoring` — its runnable floor governs what
may be deferred. Of the five, **the want is the one worth carrying into a stub**,
because it is what lets a stub act rather than merely react.

## Interaction With Connectable Authoring

The contradiction and the pressure behavior are where a connectable character's
flaws actually live. Apply `rule_connectable_companions` to both: point the armor
**outward**, at the character's problems or past, not at the protagonist. A
contradiction whose net effect is that nobody can reach them is the isolating
shape that rule warns against — redesign it so the difficulty sits in the
interior of the relationship rather than at its formation.

---

## Coverage

This rule covers the personality layer only. Appearance, knowledge boundaries,
relationships, current state, and any content-rated or intimacy layer a consuming
work defines are their own fields, authored under their own rules. A consuming
work may add personality-adjacent domains this rule says nothing about; nothing
here forbids that, and nothing here should be read as the complete set of things
a character sheet may contain.
