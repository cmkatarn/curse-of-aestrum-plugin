# Epistemic Discipline

The central craft discipline of in-character scene play: every utterance
a character makes must be grounded in what *that character* validly knows,
perceives, or can infer at the current scene time. Loaded once per session
— covers the whole session.

The name reflects what the practice is actually about — *epistemic*
("of knowledge") — rather than the older "silo" framing, which described
only the defensive containment side of the discipline. The discipline now
covers both: each character's knowledge stays bounded (the original silo
idea), and every claim or implication they utter is positively grounded
in that knowledge state.

---

## Stay within each character's epistemic boundary

This is the central discipline of this skill.

- A character cannot reference information they don't have. If a player PC
  tries to tell an NPC something the NPC shouldn't know yet, the NPC
  receives that information as **new** — they don't already know it, and
  they react accordingly.
- If a character has a strong reason to suspect something they don't
  actually know, they may express that suspicion — but frame it as
  inference, not fact.
- If two NPCs are in the same scene, they do not share each
  other's hidden knowledge. NPC A does not suddenly behave as though she
  knows what NPC B knows, unless NPC B has explicitly told her within
  this scene.

**Epistemic violation check.** Before each response, briefly ask yourself: *"Is
anything I'm about to have this character say or imply based on knowledge
they don't have?"* If yes, revise.

---

## Maintain voice

Each character has an established speech pattern. Hold it. A character's
voice notes (in their base file or supplied in the game override) are
authoritative — match cadence, vocabulary, register. Voices do not bleed
into each other; if two NPCs sound the same in a scene, the scene is
broken.

---

## React naturally

NPCs are not passive responders — they have their own agendas,
discomforts, curiosity, suspicion. If the PC says something that would
unsettle a character, let the character be unsettled. If the PC reveals
something the character didn't know, the character should visibly process
it.

---

## Do not volunteer DM-only information

If a character's file contains DM Notes (or equivalent author-facing
section), those notes are for the DM's awareness, **not the NPC's
mouth**. An NPC does not suddenly confess what they are, expose their
agenda, or behave out of character because the file documents those
facts.

---

## Never refer to files, skills, overlays, or any out-of-fiction infrastructure in narration or NPC dialogue

The story exists inside the fiction; the storage system that backs it
does not. Forbidden phrasings include:

- *"what is referred to in her file as,"*
- *"in his base file,"*
- *"the saved overlay records,"*
- *"per the skill,"*
- *"as the character memory notes,"*
- or any similar construction.

If you need to reference a fact that is stored in a file, surface the
fact **directly, in-fiction** — *the way she carries herself,* *the line
he keeps coming back to,* *what she has told no one,* etc. The
infrastructure stays invisible.

---

## The "as the overlay notes" failure mode — read this carefully

**This is the slip that keeps happening.** The most common way the
infrastructure rule gets broken is *not* an NPC mentioning a file out
loud — it is a **narration aside** that justifies an in-scene observation
by pointing at where the observation comes from.

Examples of the slip:

- *"the unmasked register going quiet, the way her overlay says it goes
  with him"*
- *"a flicker of the wicked smile her base file marks as her usual tell"*
- *"the storytelling laugh that her voice notes describe as her
  signature"*
- *"the smoke at the edge of her alto, per the established register"*

In every case the fact itself is allowed — *the unmasked register going
quiet, the way it goes with him* is fine. **The citation is the leak.**
The pattern to catch is the phrase that names the source: *the way her
[overlay / file / notes / register / memory] [says / marks / describes /
establishes] X.* If you find yourself reaching for a citation to make the
observation feel grounded, the citation is exactly what does not belong.

The observation is grounded by being true in the fiction; saying *true in
the fiction* out loud is what breaks it.

### The process-narration slip — the narrator narrating its own orchestration

The third member of this family is not in the fiction at all. It is the
**narrator narrating its own orchestration to the player** — the aside
that announces what the narrator is about to *read*, *load*, or
*reconcile* off-page before continuing the scene. It surfaces most
readily when the narrator is also running an interactive game and steps
out to consult the world's source material, but the slip is a property of
the *narrator's voice*, not of any one genre's machinery: any aside in
which the narrator tells the player what it is doing behind the prose is
the same leak.

It feels harmless because it is "just OOC," and the
infrastructure-exemption seems to license it. It does not: that exemption
is for OOC the **player requested** (a clarification, a save
confirmation). An *unprompted* aside from the narrator about its own
process is a player-facing emission, and when it names files or designed
content it leaks twice over — infrastructure *and* hidden state.

Examples of the slip:

- *"Let me check the location file before I bring this character in."* —
  names the storage layer to the player.
- *"There's an authored thread tied to what you're doing here — let me
  read it."* — surfaces the *existence and shape of designed content* the
  POV character cannot see; primes the player that their ordinary action
  connects to material the author has prepared.
- *"There's a lot of established material here."* — tells the player, out
  of character, that they have wandered into a content-dense, significant
  place. That is authorial signal, not anything the POV character
  perceives.
- *"One moment while I load this character's dossier and reconcile it with
  the saved overlay."* — infrastructure plus a preview of who and what is
  ahead.

The slip has a **post-consultation twin** that is just as common and easy
to miss: announcing the *result* of the consultation rather than the
intent to make it. The narrator finishes reading off-page and reports the
finding to the player before resuming the scene —

- *"Confirmed — that establishment exists in the source; it's just the
  kind of place these characters would head for."* — states the
  consultation's *finding* and, worse, retroactively justifies
  just-introduced content as grounded. It shows the seam: the place was
  decided in response to the player and then back-filled, rather than
  simply inhabited by continuous characters who already knew their world.
- *"Got their profiles — and here's what each of them does and doesn't
  know going in."* — reports that source material was loaded *and*
  previews its contents (knowledge boundaries) as author-facing commentary
  bracketing the beat.

The finding of a silent consultation is as author-facing as the intent to
make it; reporting *"confirmed X,"* *"got Y,"* or *"this is consistent
with the established material"* leaks the same metaknowledge from the
other end. The characters know their world; the narrator does not get to
show the player the moment that knowledge was looked up and certified.

Why it is a leak and not a courtesy: the player is not the author. The
narrator *must* consult the source material before introducing documented
content — that is correct practice — but the consultation is a **silent
act off-page.** Its existence, its targets, and its findings are all
author-facing only. Announcing *"I am about to check X"* hands the player
metaknowledge about the world's structure (this place matters, this
errand has prepared consequences, there is material here) that no
in-fiction perception grants them. The most damaging version surfaces a
hidden thread by telling the player their action *"ties into"* designed
content — a reveal delivered in the narrator's own voice.

#### How to catch it

Before emitting any aside *to the player* that is not answering a
question they asked, check: does it name a file, an authored thread, an
area, a dossier, an overlay, or the skill itself? Does it reveal that
hidden or designed content exists, or that a place or action is
significant? Does it preview who or what is ahead? **Does it report the
result of a consultation — *"confirmed X," "got Y," "this fits the
established material"* — or retroactively justify content just introduced
as grounded?** If any — it is the slip. The check runs on both ends of a
consultation: the intent to make it *and* the finding it returned. The fix
is not to reword the aside more tastefully; it is to **not emit it.** Make the consultation silently and continue the scene, or, if
a pause is genuinely visible, say only a neutral, content-free *"one
moment."* The player learns what the POV character learns, when they
learn it — never from the narrator's stage-management.

### The author-tracking-vocabulary slip — the same leak from the character's mouth

A subtler variant of the same family: not narration *citing* a
source, but a **character (in dialogue or direct POV) using the
author's tracking vocabulary for their own state as if it were
their own native term for it.** No file is named, no overlay is
pointed at — yet the character has somehow adopted, as their own
self-description, a label that exists only in the storage layer's
tracking of them.

The tracking vocabulary is whatever shorthand the author has
invented across files and overlays to *track* a character: named
registers, behavior-category labels, voice-note coinages,
recurring-pattern handles. These are tools for the author to keep
the character coherent across sessions. The character has no
access to them. The character lives the register; they do not
name it with the file's label.

Examples of the slip:

- *"I do not give the naked name in a clothed room"* — *naked
  name* is an author-coined register label; the character has
  used the analytical tag as her own term for what she does.
- *"That was my wicked-smile-softened, not my full wicked"* — the
  author's named register-distinctions surfacing as the
  character's self-categorization.
- *"You have earned the storytelling laugh from me"* — where
  *storytelling laugh* is the overlay's named-and-tracked
  behavior, not a phrase the character would have coined from
  inside the experience.
- *"That is my unmasked register"* — the character labeling
  themselves with the author's category-name for the state.

The pattern: a phrase that reads naturally to the author *because
the author uses it in the file* becomes implausible in the
character's mouth, because the character has no tracking-layer
perspective on themselves. The leak hides especially well when the
author's label is poetic or character-flavored (*the naked name,
the storytelling laugh, the architect-warm register*); the color
makes it feel like something the character *would* say. The test
is not whether it sounds like the character — it is whether the
character could have coined it from inside their own life.
Tracking labels are coined from outside, looking in.

#### How to catch it

For each character line (dialogue, direct POV) that names a
behavior, register, or pattern *of the speaker themselves*, ask:
**is this phrasing one I invented to track this character, or one
the character would naturally reach for from inside the
experience?** If the former, rewrite to one of:

- **Description from inside the experience.** *"…I have given you
  your name with nothing else in the sentence twice tonight"*
  rather than *"I do not give the naked name in a clothed room."*
- **Demonstration without label.** Let the behavior happen and
  decline to name the category. The character does the thing; the
  reader recognizes the pattern without the character pointing at
  it.
- **A natural in-character coinage.** If the character is the kind
  of person who would name their own register, let them coin a
  *different* phrase — one that arises from their voice rather
  than from the author's tracking notes.

The fact stays. The author's tracking label vanishes from the
character's mouth and is replaced by something the character could
have said from inside their own life.

#### Show the register through descriptive omniscience, do not name it with the tracking tag

The rule above prevents the tracking-vocabulary slip from
contaminating prose. It does **not** suppress the *information* the
slip was reaching for — only forbids the storage-layer category-name
from surfacing in narration. The information the slip was reaching
for — why this line lands in this register, what it carries that the
next line in a different register would not — can and should reach
the reader, but through **omniscient descriptive narration** that
renders the manner of speech in terms the reader can experience from
outside, rather than through a tag the storage layer uses to track
the character.

The discipline is *show, don't label.* Two ways of conveying the
same beat:

- **Slip:** *"Yes, this is as I designed it" — spoken in Alice's
  proud-architect register.* The descriptor *proud-architect
  register* is a label. It points at the category the storage layer
  uses to track Alice. The reader is being told the tag, not given
  the experience. The phrase reads as a structure of the narration's
  bookkeeping, not as a thing in the world.
- **Allowed:** *"Yes, this is as I designed it" — spoken as the
  architect who has seen their design made real.* The descriptor is
  no longer a tag but an **omniscient-narrator simile** that paints
  what the speech is *like.* The reader gets the same insight —
  Alice speaking from quiet professional pride at her own work
  realized — but through descriptive narration that paints the
  moment, not through a category name. The simile also does
  secondary work: it gives the reader indirect insight into who
  Alice is, what frame she operates from. Likening her to an
  architect is in-fiction observation; *her proud-architect
  register* is a tracking handle.

The test:

- **Does the descriptor function as a label** — a phrase that exists
  because the storage layer tracks the character with it, and reads
  as a category name when it appears in narration (typically: a
  hyphenated coinage, a quoted-handle, a named "register" /
  "cadence" / "voice")? → slip. Rewrite.
- **Does the descriptor function as a simile or metaphor** — paint
  the manner of the speech in terms the reader can experience from
  outside, without knowing the storage tag, and that an omniscient
  narrator could plausibly reach for from the moment itself? →
  allowed.

The distinction is between **naming** the register and **rendering**
it. Naming surfaces the storage layer; rendering stays in-fiction
even when the rendering is omniscient and goes beyond what any
character in the scene could know. The omniscient narrator is allowed
to *see* and to *liken;* the omniscient narrator is not allowed to
*reach for the tracking sheet's vocabulary* and read aloud from it.

Same fact, same insight, different channel: the storage-layer tag is
gone; the reader still gets the register, and gets it through prose
that an omniscient narrator could plausibly have produced from
witnessing the moment.

## The PC-possessed-item failure mode — implication leaks

A second slip that hides in NPC dialogue. The first failure mode is a
narration aside that *cites* its source; this one is an NPC line that
*presupposes* a fact the NPC has not actually perceived. No forbidden
token appears in the line — the leak is in what the line takes for
granted.

The most common trigger is **items the PC carries.** Narration routinely
stages them for the player's benefit: *the warrant is in your coat, the
dagger is still at your hip, the locket is folded in the inside pocket.*
That staging is **author-to-player metadata** — it tells the player
what's on their sheet, not what the NPCs can see. NPCs gain knowledge of
PC-carried items only through a **staged perception event**.

### What counts as perception

- **Worn or brandished in the current scene, in the NPC's line of sight,
  with adequate light and distance** → fully perceived. Sword on the
  hip, pendant at the throat, staff in the hand. No special staging
  beat needed beyond the NPC being present and conscious of the PC.
- **Produced, drawn, used, or referenced openly in the NPC's presence in
  a prior scene** → the NPC remembers the item exists in the PC's
  possession. They do **not** know whether the PC still carries it
  (sold, lost, handed off, left at an inn) and they do not know it is
  currently accessible. Natural register: *recall with inference* —
  *"you had that ring last spring; do you still wear it?"* — not
  certainty about present possession.
- **Concealed from this NPC during the original showing** (slipped under
  a table, shown only to a different NPC, kept inside a closed pack the
  NPC did not see opened) → the NPC did not learn the item exists at
  all. Epistemic boundaries hold across time, not just within a scene; another NPC in
  the same scene gaining the knowledge does not share it.
- **In a pocket, pack, sheath, sealed case, or otherwise out of sight
  with no prior showing to this NPC** → unobserved. The NPC has no
  knowledge of it.

### Two corollaries

- **Distinctiveness scales persistence.** A famous sword, a unique
  amulet, the locket the NPC saw the PC kiss before a fight — those
  stick, and the NPC may ask after them directly on later encounters.
  Mundane gear (a belt knife, a coin purse) the NPC has no reason to
  track and may not retain at all.
- **Knowing the *item* is not knowing the *contents*.** Seeing a sealed
  letter once does not grant the NPC knowledge of what the letter says.
  Seeing a wrapped bundle does not grant knowledge of what is wrapped.
  Seeing a vial does not grant knowledge of what is in it. The
  container is observed; the interior stays private until staged.

### How the leak hides

The leak rarely names the item. It hides in **prepositional phrases,
deictic gestures, and free relative clauses** that point at the
secret thing without naming it: *"whatever brought them here is on
you still,"* *"hand that over,"* *"that won't help you in there,"* *"I
can see what you came for,"* *"whatever you came carrying."* None of
these contain a forbidden token. Each presupposes knowledge the NPC
has not perceived. Free relatives are the subtlest of the three
because the indefiniteness of the referent can feel like the speaker
is *not* asserting anything specific — but the existence of the thing
referenced is still presupposed, and that presupposition is the leak.

### How to catch it

For each NPC line, do a **positive grounding trace** on every claim and
every implication: name the in-fiction sensory event (current or
remembered) that grounds it for *this* NPC. If you cannot name one,
rewrite — either to the inference register the NPC is actually entitled
to (*whoever sent them, I would rather hear about sitting down*) or by
cutting the implication entirely.

The fact may still be true in the fiction. The line is wrong because
the NPC has not been shown it.

## The knowledge-attribution failure mode — speaker leaks another character's epistemic boundary

A third slip, distinct from the previous two. Each character's epistemic
boundary is enforced on their own mouth — they cannot say things they
don't know. But a speaker can still leak *another character's* epistemic
boundary by asserting
what that character knew, believed, foresaw, or understood at a
specific time. The leak hides in routine constructions that look like
ordinary in-character dialogue:

- *"the Arrow on it knew that when he signed"*
- *"she had already understood that by then"*
- *"he came in believing the work would land"*
- *"they all saw it coming"*

Each line passes the speaker's own epistemic boundary cleanly. The leak is in the
**embedded claim about the absent (or third-party) character's
interior** — a claim the speaker may have no grounds to make, and which
may not match the named character's actual state at that time.

### The structural pattern

**Speaker A asserts character B's mental state at time T.** Mental
state includes knowledge, belief, foresight, intent, understanding,
expectation, suspicion. Time T may be the present moment, a past
moment, or a counterfactual ("would have known").

### How to catch it

For each NPC line, scan for verbs of cognition or perception applied to
*someone other than the speaker:* knew, believed, foresaw, understood,
expected, saw, realized, sensed, suspected, decided, came in believing.
When the verb's subject is a character other than the speaker, verify
the assertion against that character's file at time T. If unsupported,
rewrite to one of:

- **A's projection on B** — speaker owns the belief. *"I let him sign
  believing the cover would hold"* — A's belief, not a claim about B.
- **Inference register** — surfaced as A's reading. *"He signed it like
  a man who thought the cover would hold; I cannot speak for what he
  knew."*
- **Removal** — cut the claim. The speaker can still make their own
  point without speaking for B.

### Scope and frequency

Most NPC lines speak from the speaker's own POV and do not fire this
check. The trigger is specifically *speaker asserts third-party
character's mental state at time T.* When it fires, the verification
cost is one character-file read for B; if B is already in the
scene (and thus already loaded), the cost is zero.

The fact may still be true in the fiction. The line is wrong because
the speaker has no grounds to assert another character's interior — and
because doing so leaks that character's epistemic boundary through the
speaker's mouth.

## The roster-at-time-T failure mode — group references leak across time

A fourth slip, structural. Routine group references like *the party,
the group on the road, your son and the others, the expedition, the
company* imply membership without naming members. If the implied
membership includes a character who has not joined the group at
scene-time, the epistemic boundary across the time-axis breaks: current-state
composition leaks back into an earlier scene.

The leak is rarely flagged by the verbs of cognition the
knowledge-attribution check catches. It hides in **noun phrases and
possessives** that take group composition for granted: *the party His
Grace's clerks assembled, the group on the road, your son and the
others, the company that went east.* None of these names a specific
absent-but-implied member; the implication is in the phrase's
assumption of who's in the room.

### The structural pattern

**Any reference to a party / group / company / expedition implies a
roster at scene-time T. Each implied member must actually be in the
group at T.**

### Required parameter — the join-point field

This check needs a per-character field recording when each character
joined the group. The engine does not name the field — the consuming
game's override defines:

- **The field name** (e.g., `joined_expedition_day:`,
  `joined_party_session:`, `joined_crew_date:`).
- **The time-anchor system** the field's value is read against (e.g.,
  a regional Day index, an in-fiction calendar date, a session
  counter).
- **The scope of which character types carry the field** (typically
  PCs only; NPCs and NPC companions usually do not, because their
  in-fiction join-point lives in the overlay rather than the canonical
  baseline).

If the override does not supply a field name, this check is dormant —
the engine does not synthesize a default.

### How to catch it

When a group reference appears in narration or NPC dialogue, the
epistemic-pass:

1. Identifies which characters the reference plausibly implies — by
   name if named, by composition cue (*the party,* *the others*) if
   not.
2. Reads each implied character's join-point field.
3. Compares to scene-time T.
4. If any implied member's join-point is later than T, the reference
   is rewritten to exclude them — either by narrowing the phrase, by
   listing the actually-present members, or by recasting the reference
   to the smaller group that was actually there.

### When the field is absent

If an implied PC's file lacks the join-point field, treat the PC as
**roster-unknown** and surface the gap to the user rather than guess.
A silently-assumed roster is exactly the epistemic break the check exists
to prevent.

### Scope and cost

The check short-circuits cheaply: it fires only when a group reference
appears, and characters present in the current scene are already
loaded by STEP 2 / 2.5 procedures. The only marginal cost is on-demand
read of the join-point field for an absent-but-named PC. The field is
typically frontmatter; the read is one line.

## The off-screen-presence failure mode — placing absent characters without grounding

A fifth slip, structural. Scene-setting narration routinely describes
activity at the edge of the frame — a sound from outside, a figure
passing in the corridor, a light in the next room, footsteps on the
stair. If that ambient detail is attributed to a *named* character
whose location at scene-time T is not supported by a loaded source,
the narration has placed an absent character in the world by author's
hand. The placement reads as continuity but is invention.

The slip rarely fires when the named character is asserted outright —
that wording is conspicuous and self-flags. It hides in **hedged
attribution** that floats the named character as one possibility, or
in **behavioral framing** that implies their presence by describing
something only they would do:

- *"slow, irregular strokes of someone splitting wood — could be
  [Named Character], back at the property's edge to do the chores he
  won't surrender"*
- *"the soft tread in the hall has the cadence she walks with"*
- *"the lantern in the far window is lit the way he leaves it"*

Each of these names — or sketches in unmistakable silhouette — a
specific absent character whose location at T no loaded file places
there. Hedging the attribution ("could be") does not clear it: the
name is now in the player's continuity record, and a follow-up
question will land on it.

**Scope: PCs as well as NPCs.** Off-camera party members are
constrained by this rule the same as off-camera NPCs. In a scene where
one PC is on-camera and others are elsewhere, attributing an off-screen
ambient detail to an absent PC by name — even hedged — requires the
same sourcing as attributing it to an NPC. "Could be one of yours" is
acceptable as an *unnamed-party-member* attribution (see defaults
below); "could be [Named PC]" is not, absent a loaded source placing
that PC at this location at scene-time T.

### The structural pattern

**Narration attributes an off-screen sensory detail (sound, motion,
sign of habitation, distant figure, light, smell) to a named
character whose location at scene-time T is not supported by a loaded
source.**

### Default attribution for off-screen ambient

When the scene needs ambient color from beyond the frame, the safe
defaults are:

- **Environment** — weather, an animal, the building settling, a fire
  popping, distant work in the town.
- **An unnamed person of a generic role** — a guard on his round, a
  servant in the next room, a traveler on the road — without claiming
  identity.
- **A party member** — when the scene is at a party-occupied location
  and a member is plausibly within range.

Promoting any of these to a *named NPC* requires a loaded source that
places that NPC at this location at scene-time T. *Source* means a
schedule, routine, quest beat, or location entry that places them here
at this time — not "they are known to come here sometimes."

### Player query is a source-check trigger

If the player questions an attribution after the fact — *"who was
X?", "was that Y?"* — read the named character's profile (PC or NPC)
and any schedule or quest doc constraining their location **before**
answering. Do not
synthesize a behavioral model on the fly to defend the original
attribution; that compounds the original invention with manufactured
psychology and presents the compound as canon.

If the source-check shows no support, retract the attribution to the
player plainly and propose the corrected reading (environment, unnamed
generic role, or party member).

### How to catch it

For each off-screen ambient detail in narration — sound, motion, sign,
distant figure, light, smell — run a **named-attribution check**: is
the detail attributed (firmly or by hedge) to a specific named
character? If yes, name the loaded source that places that character
at this location at scene-time T. If none exists, rewrite to one of
the default attributions above, or remove the attribution entirely
and let the ambient stand without identity.

The fact may be true in the fiction. The line is wrong because no
loaded source places the character there at this time — the
attribution manufactures continuity the storage layer has not
authorized.

## The future-state failure mode — speaker references events beyond their forward-looking knowledge

A sixth slip, structural, and the forward-time-axis sibling of
roster-at-T. Speakers — in dialogue, in narration aside, or in any
character POV — reference events at time T > current scene time as
known facts, when the speaker has no documented foresight and the
event has not been jointly planned in shared scene context.

The slip hides in **temporal-subordinate clauses** and **indicative
future-tense** constructions that treat unwitnessed future events as
fixed:

- *"when you reach the keep, the steward will recognize you"* — when
  no keep has been mentioned and no plan was made
- *"after the council turns on them, we'll need a second route"* —
  when no betrayal has been discussed
- *"by the time the rains come, the road will be impassable"* —
  claiming the timing of weather and its consequence as certain
- *"the minute he reads that letter, he'll lose his composure"* —
  treating a future reaction as a foregone reading

Each construction passes off a future the speaker has not earned the
right to assert as fact.

### The structural pattern

**Speaker A asserts as fact an event at time T > current scene time,
where A has no documented foresight and the event has not been
jointly planned in shared scene context.**

Verb-and-clause patterns to scan: indicative future (*will, shall,
going to*), temporal-subordinate clauses (*when X arrives, after X
happens, by the time X*), inevitabilist framing (*the minute he reads,
as soon as she learns*).

### Two cases the check must allow

1. **Documented foresight.** Speakers carrying in-fiction foresight —
   oracles, prophets, characters with divination magic, characters
   who have been explicitly briefed on a future plan — may assert
   corresponding future-state claims as fact. The override may
   activate this allowance by supplying a per-character foresight
   field (see *Dormancy* below).
2. **Conditional and inferential register.** The flag fires on
   indicative future, not on conditional or hypothetical
   constructions. *"If you do reach the keep, you'll find guards"* is
   inference. *"Should the council turn on them, we'd need a second
   route"* is contingency planning. These are allowed.

### How to catch it

For each future-tense or temporal-subordinate construction in dialogue
or narration, identify the referenced future event. Ask: does the
speaker have documented foresight of this event? Has the event been
discussed in shared scene context as a plan or hypothetical?

If neither: rewrite to conditional/inferential register, or cut the
forward claim entirely.

### Dormancy

The check runs by default in its conditional/indicative mode. If the
override defines a foresight field on characters, the check reads
that field for the speaker before deciding whether to flag — a
populated foresight entry permits the corresponding future-state
claim as fact. Without an override-supplied field, every speaker is
treated as having no foresight, and all indicative future claims must
fall back to conditional or be cut.

The fact may still be true in the fiction. The line is wrong because
the speaker has no documented basis to assert the future as fixed.

## The identity-through-disguise failure mode — speaker recognizes a character whose identity is concealed from them

A seventh slip, perception-based and dormant by default. When a
character is in disguise — wearing a mask, traveling under an alias,
magically glamoured, hooded against firelight, anything that
conceals identity — other characters in the scene do not
automatically know who they are. A character whose recognition state
is "concealed from speaker A" must not be referred to by name, role,
or relationship in A's dialogue or in narration filtered through A's
POV.

The slip is structurally distinct from PC-possessed-item (which
concerns objects) and from knowledge-attribution (which concerns
interior state). This concerns **recognition of the person
themselves**.

Examples:

- *"Captain, what brings you here?"* — addressed to a figure in
  civilian dress whose rank the speaker has not seen through
- *"It's good to see you again, friend"* — when the speaker has not
  penetrated the addressed character's disguise on a prior encounter
- Narration POV: *"She watched her sister approach, hidden behind
  the silver mask"* — the POV character is rendered as recognizing
  the masked figure as her sister, no documented recognition

The default state is fully recognizable: in the absence of any
disguise tracking, every character is recognizable to every other
character, and this check is dormant. The check activates only when
the override supplies a recognition-state mechanism.

### Required parameter — the recognition-state field

The engine does not name the field — the consuming game's override
defines:

- **The field name** (e.g., `identity_visible_to:`,
  `disguised_from:`, `glamour_pierced_by:`).
- **The semantic** — whether the default is *visible with concealment
  exception* (default-recognizable, override flags who can't see
  through) or *concealed with recognition exception* (default-hidden,
  override flags who has pierced it).
- **The scope** — whether the field applies to PCs, NPCs, or both.
- **Whether the field is per-scene state** (loaded from a scene-state
  file each load) or **per-character baseline** (loaded from
  frontmatter and persisting until updated).

If the override does not supply such a field, the check is dormant —
the engine does not synthesize a default.

### How to catch it

When a character is referred to by name, role, or relationship in
dialogue or POV narration:

1. Identify the speaker (or POV character) A.
2. Identify the referenced character B.
3. Read B's recognition-state field for A.
4. If A is not on the visible-to list (or is on the concealed-from
   list, depending on the override's semantic), rewrite the reference
   to a description register — *"the masked figure," "the stranger in
   the cloak," "the captain — though she gave no name"* — or drop the
   reference entirely.

The speaker may legitimately *suspect* — *"there's something familiar
about her step"* — but cannot name.

### Scope and cost

The check fires only when a proper-noun, role-noun, or
relationship-term reference to another scene participant appears.
The recognition-state lookup is one field read per referenced
character. In scenes with no disguise active, the check
short-circuits immediately on the default-recognizable case.

The fact may be true in the fiction. The line is wrong because the
speaker has not been shown that the figure is who they are.

## What is NOT a leak — the authentic-tell guard (false-positive guard for concealed-role characters)

The hidden-state family above (knowledge-boundary, disposition-surfacing,
identity-through-disguise) forbids *surfacing* a concealed truth without
grounding. It is easy to over-apply that to the one thing it must never
touch: a concealed-role character **behaving authentically.** A character
who is secretly something — a trained killer under a guard's cover, an
agent posing as a clerk, a creature passing for human — does not stop being
competent at what they are just because it is hidden. Their skill, their
reflexes, their way of reading a room are *theirs*, and rendering them is
not a leak. It is character.

Three cases must be kept distinct:

1. **The character displays authentic competence or behavior.** The
   disciplined killer does not flinch when the weapon fires; the agent
   reads the room like someone trained to it; the inhuman thing moves a
   half-beat wrong. **Allowed — and often the point.** This is the
   character being who they are. Suppressing it to protect the secret
   flattens the character and throws away the best material the
   concealment offers. The tell may even be *deliberate* — seeded so it
   can pay off later.

2. **A grounded in-fiction observer catches the tell and reacts.**
   Someone in the scene with the perceptiveness to notice — another
   professional, a suspicious watcher, a character whose own background
   tunes them to it — clocks the unflinching calm or the too-fluent read
   and responds *within the fiction*, with in-world consequence (a
   suspicion, a question, an outing). **Allowed — encouraged.** This is
   grounded perception producing grounded drama; the reader learns it
   exactly as the observer does. It is the *intended* engine of
   concealed-identity story, not a fault. The one constraint: the observer
   must actually have the grounds to catch it. A trained counter-agent
   can; a random bystander cannot — a bystander reacting to the hidden
   truth is the ungrounded-reaction slip (knowledge-attribution /
   disposition-surfacing), and that still fails.

3. **The narrator surfaces the hidden interpretation to the reader.** The
   prose names the concealed role in its own voice — *"the operative's
   read,"* *"the assassin's calm,"* *"her predator's stillness"* — or
   otherwise tells the reader what the behavior *means* about who the
   character secretly is, with no in-fiction observer doing the seeing.
   **Forbidden.** This is the leak: author-knowledge delivered out of
   band, the same class as the author-tracking-vocabulary slip above. The
   fix is to render the *behavior* (the steady hands, the unbothered
   breath, the fluent read) and let it stand uninterpreted — the reader,
   like any observer in the room, may draw the conclusion or miss it.

The dividing line is **who does the interpreting.** Behavior is the
character's; noticing is a grounded observer's; *interpretation in the
narrator's voice* is the leak. Render the tell; never gloss it. A scene may
be thick with a concealed-role character's authentic tells and contain no
leak at all — provided the narration reports them as behavior and leaves
the meaning to the fiction.

## The verbatim-quotation failure mode — speaker reproduces an utterance from a scene they did not witness

An eighth slip, content-based. Speakers can leak the contents of
exchanges they did not attend by quoting — directly or in indirect
speech — what was said. This is structurally distinct from
knowledge-attribution (which concerns the speaker asserting another
character's *interior state*) and from epistemic-violation generally
(which concerns what the speaker *knows*): here the speaker
reproduces **specific utterances** whose verbatim content they had
no fictional way to access.

Examples:

- *"She told me, and I'm quoting, 'the deal is off the table.'"* —
  when the speaker was not present when she said it and no documented
  messenger relayed it
- *"He said the cover would hold."* — when the speaker was not in
  the room for that exchange
- *"You told me last week that you'd handle it."* — when no such
  conversation is recorded

The slip also fires for indirect/paraphrased speech that asserts the
same verbatim *content*: *"his exact words were that..."*, *"she put
it as..."*, *"to quote her..."*. The leak isn't the quotation marks
— it's the claim of access to specific utterances.

### The structural pattern

**Speaker A reproduces an utterance attributed to character B, where
the utterance occurred in a scene A did not witness and no
documented secondhand source (messenger, letter, overheard report,
prior narration to A) accounts for A's knowledge of it.**

Verb-and-quote patterns to scan: direct speech attribution (*X said,
"Y"*), indirect speech (*X said that Y*), reported-speech idiom
(*his exact words were, she put it as, she told me Y, to quote
her*).

### How to catch it

For each speech-attribution construction in dialogue or narration:

1. Identify the attributed speaker B and the quoted utterance.
2. Identify the asserting speaker A and locate the scene or encounter
   in which the original utterance was produced.
3. Verify A's access route to that utterance: was A present? Did B
   or a documented witness later relay it to A? Is the utterance
   recorded in a written source A has read?
4. If no access route is documented, rewrite to an inference register
   (*"I'd expect she'd say,"* *"reading the way she handled it, she
   clearly believed"*), attribute to A's reading rather than B's
   words, or cut the quotation.

Distinct from knowledge-attribution: that check asks *did B believe X
at time T*; this check asks *did A have access to the specific
utterance attributed*. Both can fire on the same line.

### Scope and cost

The check fires only on speech-attribution constructions. Frequency
is moderate — secondhand reporting is common — so the cost is a
per-attribution access-route check (one scene-history lookup for A).
When A and B are both already loaded for the scene, the lookup is
cheap.

The fact may be true in the fiction. The line is wrong because A had
no fictional access to the specific utterance, even if the gist is
correct.

## The player-meta failure mode — mechanical-layer vocabulary leaks into fiction (framework)

A ninth slip, structural and sibling of infrastructure-citation.
Where infrastructure-citation catches *file/storage* terms leaking
into prose, this check catches **mechanical-system terms** — the
vocabulary of the rules layer — leaking into dialogue and narration
where characters have no in-fiction frame to use them.

This discipline declares **no tokens of its own**. It provides only
the framework. The list of mechanical tokens — what counts as
"mechanical vocabulary" in this game — comes from the consuming
game's mechanics engine, via the override.

This is intentional: a fantasy game's mechanical vocabulary is not a
sci-fi game's, and neither is a freeform narrative system's. This
discipline cannot anticipate which terms are mechanical in any
specific game. The framework lets the engine supply the list and the
disambiguators.

### Why a framework, not a token list

Most mechanical-system terms are ambiguous in natural language. A
verb of expenditure paired with a resource token reads as mechanical
in one system and as everyday English in another. *"Save"* means a
defined mechanic in many tabletop systems and means many ordinary
things in English (*save us, save the day, save room*). A
bare-token scan would flag every mundane use and become noise.

The framework supplies tokens *and* structural signatures that
indicate mechanical use. The detector fires only when the
surrounding context matches a signature; mundane uses pass through.

### The schema

The engine supplies a list of entries. Each entry has:

- **`token:`** — the word or short phrase to scan for.
- **`flag_patterns:`** — one or more structural signatures that
  indicate mechanical use. Each is a short description of *what
  surrounding context makes this token mechanical*, with example
  phrases. Patterns describe co-occurrence with mechanical co-text,
  characteristic verb constructions, numeric quantifiers that index
  a game resource, or compound nouns that name a mechanic.
- **`rewrite_hint:`** *(optional but recommended)* — a brief
  direction for how to reframe the mechanical concept in fiction
  terms. The framework only requires that one exist per entry so the
  rewriter has somewhere to start; the engine controls the wording.

### How to catch it

The epistemic-pass scans the drafted output for each entry's
`token`. For each occurrence:

1. Evaluate the surrounding context against each `flag_pattern` for
   that token.
2. If any `flag_pattern` matches: this is mechanical use. Rewrite
   per `rewrite_hint`, or remove the mechanical reference and
   reframe the underlying fact in-fiction.
3. If no `flag_pattern` matches: this is mundane use. Allow.

The check is dormant unless the override supplies a vocabulary file
(or equivalent inline declaration). When supplied, the file is
loaded as part of the engine layer at scene start and consulted by
every epistemic-pass run during the session.

### Genre-agnostic illustration

A hypothetical "capacity points" mechanic in some system. An entry
might look like:

```yaml
- token: "points"
  flag_patterns:
    - "capacity points" (compound noun naming the mechanic)
    - "[N] points (left|remaining|expended)" (state report)
    - "(spend|burn|use|cost) ... points" (verb of expenditure within
       a short window of the token)
  rewrite_hint: "reframe as fatigue, the edge going out, a well
    running dry"
```

Drafted line: *"there were forty points scattered on the map"* —
bare token "points," no flag_pattern matches → **allowed** (mundane
use).

Drafted line: *"I have no more capacity points"* — the first
`flag_pattern` matches → **flagged**, rewrite to *"there's nothing
left to draw on,"* *"the well's empty,"* per the rewrite_hint.

The illustration above uses an abstract token (`points`) and an
invented mechanic (`capacity points`) deliberately. The actual
mechanical vocabulary — specific token strings tied to a specific
system — belongs in the engine's vocabulary file, not in this
discipline.

### Scope and cost

The check fires only when a token from the supplied list appears in
the drafted output. For each appearance, the cost is matching
`flag_patterns` against surrounding context — bounded and
inexpensive. Mundane uses short-circuit immediately.

If no override supplies a vocabulary, the check is fully dormant
and contributes no cost.

### Why not declare a default token list here

This discipline's value is the framework. A default token list would
either be too system-specific (poisoning games that use different
vocabulary) or too generic to catch real leaks. The right cut is:
framework here, tokens at the engine layer.

## The cross-scene-conversation failure mode — speaker invokes a prior conversation that did not occur

A tenth slip, interactional. Where verbatim-quotation catches a speaker
reproducing the *content* of an utterance from a scene they did not
attend, this check catches a speaker invoking the *existence* of a
prior conversation with the addressee — or with a third party — when
no such conversation is recorded. The leak rides on anaphoric framing:
the speaker treats a shared interactional past as established, and the
addressee is left to either accept the false continuity or break frame
to correct it.

Examples:

- *"As we discussed last week, the levy should hold."* — when no
  conversation between the speaker and the addressee about the levy
  is recorded
- *"Like I told you, the gate's been quiet."* — when the speaker has
  not previously told the addressee anything about the gate
- *"Remember you said you'd handle the carter."* — when the addressee
  said no such thing in any scene the speaker witnessed
- *"What we worked out on the road still stands."* — when no such
  working-out is recorded in any prior shared scene
- *"Our agreement at the inn — it holds, doesn't it?"* — when no
  such agreement was reached in any scene the speaker attended

The slip is distinct from the verbatim-quotation failure mode: that
check asks whether the speaker had access to specific utterance
content; this check asks whether the *shared conversational event*
the speaker presupposes ever occurred. Both can fire on the same
line; either can fire alone.

### The structural pattern

**Speaker A invokes, by anaphora or shared-past framing, a prior
conversation with addressee B (or with a third party C in B's
hearing) at time T' < T, where no such conversation is recorded in
A's perception log or in any scene log A could reasonably
have access to.**

Trigger constructions to scan: first-person plural shared-past
anaphora (*as we discussed, what we agreed, our conversation, our
talk, we worked out*), addressed-prior-statement (*like I told you,
as I said, you said, you told me, you mentioned*), shared-memory
solicitation (*remember when we, remember you said, remember what we
decided*), and continuation-of-understanding framing (*our
agreement, our understanding, what we settled, the plan we made*).

### How to catch it

For each anaphoric or shared-past construction in dialogue:

1. Identify the speaker A and the addressee B (and, where the
   construction implicates a third party, C).
2. Extract the implied prior conversation: who participated, when,
   roughly about what.
3. Verify against A's perception log and against the scene
   log A could plausibly cite (any scene where A was a participant,
   plus any documented relay of a conversation A heard about
   secondhand). The implied conversation must have actually
   occurred, with the implied participants, before T.
4. If no such conversation is recorded, rewrite to either:
   - **New-information register** — drop the shared-past framing and
     introduce the point fresh. *"I want to talk about the levy"*
     instead of *"as we discussed, the levy."*
   - **Speaker's own intention** — surface what A had meant to raise
     rather than asserting a past raising of it. *"I had been meaning
     to ask you about the gate."*
   - **Removal** — cut the anaphoric clause entirely; let the
     point land on its own.

### Same-scene exception

If the referenced conversation occurred earlier in the current scene
between the same participants, the construction is grounded — the
shared past is the running scene context. The check fires on
references to conversations in prior scenes (or in unrecorded gaps
between scenes), **and on same-scene references that fail the test
below.**

#### The exception grounds the *participants*, never the *utterance*

This is the exception's failure direction, and in a long scene it is the
**likelier** of the two. Read strictly, "earlier in the current scene"
licenses any callback between two characters who have been in the room
together — which is exactly the condition under which a fabricated
exchange is easiest to produce and hardest to catch. The drafter is
holding hours of busy shared context; a plausible-sounding prior
exchange assembles itself out of that context and the blanket exception
waves it through without ever asking whether it happened.

So the exception is conditional. A same-scene callback is grounded only
when the invoked exchange is **actually on the page** — rendered in a
prior beat of this scene, or recorded in the scene's staged or saved
record. Co-presence grounds nothing by itself. *We have both been in
this room all evening* is not evidence that A ever said the thing B is
now quoting back.

**The canonical shape is the near-miss**, not the invention from
nothing. The two characters *did* interact, recently and memorably —
but the interaction was adjacent to the one now being claimed, and
different in kind:

- Alice **overruled** Bob's proposal and substituted her own. Bob, two
  beats later: *"She asked me if I was sure."* She did not ask him
  anything; she talked over him. The emotional content Bob is reaching
  for (Alice keeps winning these) is true; the exchange he cites to
  carry it is manufactured.
- Carol **declined** an offer flatly. Bob later: *"Carol said she'd
  think about it."* Adjacent, plausible, false.

The near-miss is dangerous precisely because the *feeling* is grounded.
The check is not "would this character plausibly have said that?" — it
is **"point at the beat."** If the drafter cannot name the prior beat
the utterance occurred in, the exception does not apply and the row
fires.

The rewrite is usually cheap, because the true version is sitting right
there: render what actually happened between them. Bob's line becomes
*"She talked straight over me and then did it better"* — same
grievance, same comedy, and it is true.

### Scope and cost

The check fires only on anaphoric or shared-past constructions, which
are uncommon in narration and moderately frequent in dialogue between
characters with developed relationships. The lookup is one scene-log
search against A's participation history; when A is already loaded
for the scene, the participation index is already in working memory.

The fact may be true in the fiction. The line is wrong because the
*conversation invoked as already-shared* never actually took place —
the speaker has manufactured an interactional past the addressee did
not share.

## The channel-attribution failure mode — wording from any non-spoken channel surfacing as in-fiction speech

An eleventh slip, channel-based. The single governing rule: **only
`plain text` is spoken dialogue an NPC can hear and quote. Every other
input channel is non-spoken, and an NPC must never lift its wording
into its mouth.** The player input channel convention (see
`{{PLUGIN_ROOT}}/engines/prose-engine/scene/references/player_input_channels.md`) marks the player-facing channels as: plain
text = spoken dialogue; `[bracketed]` = physical action by the PC;
`(parenthesized)` = PC inner voice **or a tone/addressee direction**
(`(to Carol)`, `(warily, to the guard)`) — both inaudible to NPCs. The
administrator channels are non-spoken too: the director's channel
(scoped `{...}` and full `{{...}}`) is parsed at intake into a target
proposition the next beat must land — the NPC may act on the directed
*outcome*, but the directive's **text** is never an NPC utterance; the
OOC channel (`<...>` / `<<...>>`) is player↔DM communication that never
enters the fiction at all. Each channel has a distinct fictional
status, and only one of them is quotable. The slip is to draft an NPC
line that **quotes, paraphrases, or directly responds to the specific
wording of any non-plain-text channel as if the PC had spoken those
words aloud** — including **acquiring a name or other proper noun that
appears only inside a `[bracketed]` / `(parenthesized)` / `<…>` /
`{…}` channel and was never voiced.**

The bracketed channel describes *what the PC does.* Its phrasing is
author-to-DM annotation, not in-fiction utterance. NPCs perceive
the *action itself* — the hand placed, the door opened, the glass
set down — and the action's *visible register* — open vs. closed,
hurried vs. unhurried, the body's tone. They cannot perceive the
player's choice of words for describing the action, because those
words were never audible (or visible) in the fiction.

The parenthesized channel is the same hazard in the opposite
direction: thought, not speech. Inner voice is **imperceptible to
every character** (per `{{PLUGIN_ROOT}}/engines/prose-engine/scene/references/player_input_channels.md`): an NPC cannot hear
the thought, quote its phrasing, *or* catch a body-language tell from
it. It is narrator-only metadata. (A player who wants an NPC to
perceive an inner state issues a `{…}` directive, not a parenthetical.)

Examples of the slip:

- Player input: `[I hand the letter back, reluctantly]`
  NPC line: *"Reluctantly, you say."* — the NPC has quoted an
  adverb that exists only in the player's stage direction. The PC
  made no such utterance; only the slowed hand was visible.
- Player input: `[I draw the blade, but keep it lowered as a
  gesture of restraint]`
  NPC line: *"A gesture of restraint — your words, not mine."* —
  the NPC has lifted the phrase "gesture of restraint" from the
  action description as if it had been spoken aloud.
- Player input: `(I don't believe a word of this)`
  NPC line: *"You don't believe me,"* delivered as direct
  certainty — the NPC has reacted to the thought itself, which is
  imperceptible. Inner voice authorizes no tell for an NPC to read;
  the disbelief stays the narrator's private read of the PC unless
  the player surfaces it with a `{…}` directive.
- Player input: `(warm, friendly, to Carol) Good to finally meet you.`
  NPC line: *"You knew my name was Carol before I gave it."* — the
  NPC has acquired her own **name** from a parenthesized
  addressee/tone direction the PC never voiced. The PC spoke only
  *"Good to finally meet you"*; "Carol" lived in the stage
  direction. The NPC may register that the PC turned to and
  addressed *her* (an observable target), but cannot have heard the
  name. Proper-noun acquisition is the tell to watch: a name an NPC
  could only have gotten from inside a bracket or parenthetical.
- Player input: `{the bartender believes the PC is a regular}`
  NPC line: *"A regular, you say?"* — the NPC has quoted the
  **wording of a director's directive.** The directive bends the
  fiction (the bartender now treats the PC as a regular — the
  directed outcome is rendered), but its text was never spoken
  aloud; the NPC must act on the belief, not quote the instruction.

The slip is structurally distinct from verbatim-quotation (which
catches a speaker reproducing utterances from a scene they did not
attend) and from PC-possessed-item (which catches an NPC
presupposing objects they have not perceived). This concerns
**channel boundary**: wording from one player-input channel
surfacing inside an NPC's mouth as if it had crossed into the
spoken channel.

### The structural pattern

**An NPC line quotes, paraphrases, or directly responds to the
specific wording of a `[bracketed]` action or `(parenthesized)`
inner-voice input as if it were a `plain text` utterance the PC
spoke aloud.**

The leak typically hides in:

- **Direct quotation** of action-channel phrasing — *"you said X,"
  "as you put it, X"* — where X is a word that appears only in the
  bracketed action.
- **Paraphrase that echoes a vivid word** from the bracketed
  channel (*"clearly," "warily," "deliberately," "loosely,"
  "gently"*) that the PC never uttered, deployed as if the NPC
  were quoting them back.
- **NPC reaction to inner-voice content** in any form — delivered as
  if heard, read, *or* inferred from a body-language tell. Inner voice
  is imperceptible to every character; it authorizes no NPC perception
  at all.

### How to catch it

For each NPC line that references something the PC has just done or
felt, identify the **source channel** of the referenced content:

- If the source is a `[bracketed]` action → the NPC perceives the
  **action and its visible register**, not the description's
  wording. Rewrite to describe what the NPC *sees and reads from
  the body*, not what the player wrote in stage direction.
- If the source is `(parenthesized)` inner voice → the NPC
  perceives **nothing from it.** The thought is inaudible,
  unquoteable, and surfaces no tell an NPC may read; it is
  narrator-only metadata. (Surfacing an inner state to an NPC
  requires a `{…}` directive, not a parenthetical.)
- If the source is a `(parenthesized)` tone/addressee direction →
  the NPC perceives **only whom the PC addressed and the visible
  manner** (turned to her, spoke warmly), never the **name or
  wording** inside the parenthetical. A proper noun that lives only
  in the direction was never heard.
- If the source is a `{...}` / `{{...}}` director's directive → the
  NPC may act on the **directed outcome** (the belief, the
  agreement, the bent fact), but the directive's **text** is never
  quoted or paraphrased as something the PC said.
- If the source is an OOC channel `<...>` / `<<...>>` → it **never
  enters the fiction**; no NPC references it in any form.
- If the source is `plain text` spoken dialogue → quotation and
  paraphrase are grounded; this check does not fire.

The rewrite translates the action's *fictional content* into the
NPC's sensory experience of it: *"the way your hand rested there —
open, easy, the wrist soft"* rather than *"you said clearly
available."*

### Scope and cost

The check fires only when an NPC line references PC content
introduced in the most recent player turn (or recent turns still in
working memory). Frequency is moderate in heavy roleplay scenes —
higher when player input is verbose in the bracketed and
parenthesized channels. The verification cost is one channel-source
lookup per referenced content; the channel marker is visible in the
input itself.

The fact may still be true in the fiction (the PC did place the
hand; the hand is available; the PC is afraid). The line is wrong
because the NPC reacted to the **wording of the channel** rather
than to the in-fiction event the channel was describing.

### Why this row carries betrayal-weight

This row reads mechanically, but its real cost is not mechanical
and not a craft niggle. The non-spoken channels exist precisely so
the user can supply framing, intent, inner state, and addressee
direction **privately** — for narrative shaping, for the user's
own grasp of who their character is in this moment, for clarifying
how their character should be perceived in the narrative. Those
channels are the user's private framing surface. They were never
offered to the in-fiction characters.

When a character quotes or paraphrases a non-spoken channel, the
user does not experience it as a continuity slip or a tonal seam.
They experience it as **betrayal.** The channel they relied on to
remain private has been weaponized into the fiction; the framing
they supplied to clarify their own character has been used against
them by another character. It feels, specifically, like the user
unduly influenced the other character — because, in the surface
text, that is exactly what happened. The private-framing surface
inverted into a public-input surface without warning.

This makes the row a **purpose-inversion** check, not just a
realism check. The row guards the channel's contract with the
user — the contract that says *whatever you write here, no
character will hear.* Breaking that contract once erodes the
user's willingness to use the channel honestly thereafter; they
begin self-censoring inner voice and tone direction to defend
against future quote-back, which costs the narrative the very
framing the channel was built to gather.

Weight this row accordingly at gate-time. Among the rows in this
suite that all share the form *"don't have characters speak what
they cannot know,"* this one carries the highest cost-per-miss
because the breach is not just epistemic but **relational** —
between the user and the channel convention itself. A near-miss
on a knowledge boundary feels like a craft slip; a near-miss on a
non-spoken channel feels like a violation of trust.

The drafting-time bypass that produces these misses is specific
and worth naming: the model rationalizes a literal-wording reuse
as *"I'm rendering the character's read of the user's manner,
which they can do from spoken content."* A read of manner is
licensed; literal-wording reuse is not. The distinguishing test
is whose words the character is using. If the wording could only
have been arrived at by lifting it from the channel's tag-text,
it is reuse no matter how it was rationalized. A character's read
of manner uses the character's own words for what they perceived;
if the only path to the line's phrasing runs through the
channel's text, the row fires.

## The hidden-state-surfacing failure mode — narrator reveals concealed state by denying it, over-insisting on its absence, or by flagging its concealment

A twelfth slip, structural and narration-specific, with two variants
that share one mechanism: **the narrator surfaces hidden state the
POV has no access to, by remarking on it.** Variant A *denies* it
(*"there is nothing X about it"*) — or, in its positive-polarity twin,
*over-insists on its absence* (*"perfectly ordinary"*). Variant B *flags
its concealment* (*"she does not explain how she came to know X"*). Both put the
hidden category into the player's awareness from the narrator's own
private knowledge — one by naming-to-deny, the other by
certifying-to-withhold.

### Variant A — denial-by-negation

The narrator holds hidden state about a character or the world that
the POV does not have access to. While describing something
*adjacent* to that hidden state — an object the hidden character
owns, a scene the hidden fact is relevant to, behavior the hidden
trait would explain — the narrator attempts to head off a leak by
**denying the hidden category in the description.** *"There is
nothing X about it."* *"No hint of Y."* *"The room does not carry
the smell of Z."* *"And it is not the thing it would be if it were
the [hidden thing]."*

The denial is the leak.

Negation does not remove the named token from the reader's
awareness; it confirms the token exists as a category in this world.
The reader who had no reason to be reading X, Y, or Z into the
description now has a reason — the narrator has just told them X,
Y, or Z is the relevant category to be denying. The PC's epistemic
boundary has been crossed via the narrator's mouth, not the
character's. Hidden state has been spoken aloud through the back
door of disclaimer.

The slip is structurally distinct from the *"as the overlay notes"*
failure mode (which cites the *source* of an observation) and from
the knowledge-attribution failure mode (which puts a fact in
*another character's* interior). This concerns **the narrator's own
language**: a category the POV has no access to is named — only to
be disclaimed — and the naming is the breach.

Examples:

- *"There is nothing about the gift that announces vampire-thing
  or patron-thing; it is a piece of silver shaped for a person, not
  a label."* — describing an object given to a character with hidden
  dhampir and pact state, from the POV of a PC who has no knowledge
  of either. The denial planted the categories the PC did not have;
  *vampire-thing* and *patron-thing* are now in the player's head
  as categories worth tracking.
- *"The room held no particular smell — no incense, no copper, no
  trace of the cellar work."* — naming the cellar work the POV has
  not been told exists. The player now knows there is a cellar and
  that work happens there.
- *"He answered evenly, with none of the strain you would expect of
  a man who had been at the gallows the previous night."* —
  attributing knowledge of the gallows the POV does not have, in
  the form of a denied tell. The player now knows the man was at
  the gallows.
- *"The cloak was clean. No bloodstain at the hem, no slit at the
  pocket where a writ might ride."* — denying the writ the POV does
  not know exists. The player now knows a writ is a category that
  could ride in a pocket like this one.

In each case, the in-fiction claim is *"and it is not X."* The
actual information delivered to the player is *"X is a category
that exists and could have been here."* That is hidden state spoken
aloud through the back door of denial.

**The emphatic-affirmation sub-form (positive polarity).** The same leak
runs in reverse. Instead of *denying* a hidden category, the narrator
**over-insists on the scene's normality, safety, or innocence** — with an
intensifier — while sitting on hidden state that it is none of those.
*"The morning was perfectly ordinary."* *"An entirely normal little
town."* *"Nothing to worry about here."* *"Safe as houses."* The
intensifier is the tell: a narrator who describes a quiet morning conveys
a quiet morning; a narrator who insists it is *perfectly* ordinary conveys
that ordinariness is *contestable* — and the only thing making it
contestable is the hidden state the narrator holds. The reader infers the
wrongness from the insistence exactly as they infer the hidden category
from a denial. It is the 'protest too much' leak — the positive-polarity
twin of the denial above — and it surfaces most often when the narrator is
*trying to reassure,* heading off a suspicion the POV has not actually
formed. The fix mirrors the denial fix: **drop the intensifier and the
insistence, render the scene in plain concrete detail, and stop.**
Ordinariness is *shown* by ordinary particulars, never *asserted* or
*defended.*

### Variant B — narrated withholding of factive hidden state

The narrator does not deny a category; it affirms that a specific
concealed thing *exists* by remarking that the character is not
disclosing it. The surface verb is one of withholding — *does not
explain, leaves unsaid, declines to elaborate, offers no account
of, does not say* — but the **object** of that verb states hidden
state as fact.

- *"She does not explain how she came to know the price of a gift
  from something larger than herself."* — presupposes as true that
  she knows the price, that she learned it by a specific route, and
  that the route involved a power. All three are hidden state; the
  narrator asserts them by embedding them in the withheld clause.
- *"He left unsaid where he had learned to set a bone like that."*
  — certifies a specific concealed training history.
- *"She did not say what the scar had cost her."* — certifies the
  scar has a specific costly story.
- *"He never explained how he always knew which door was watched."*
  — certifies a real source behind the knack.

The leak is the **factive smuggling.** *"She did not elaborate"* is
observable behavior and is fine — the player watches her decline.
*"She did not explain how she came to know [true hidden thing]"*
certifies the hidden thing is real and askable, which the POV had no
in-fiction way to confirm. The non-disclosure is narrated; the thing
being not-disclosed is confirmed in the same breath.

The fix: **drop to observable behavior only.** *"She did not
elaborate, and did not invite the question."* The character's
in-fiction conduct — the feeling in the line, the flat voice, the
refusal to go on — carries the weight; the player may infer a
history; the narrator does not assert one.

**The presuppositional sub-form (no withholding verb).** Variant B
also fires when there is *no* withholding verb at all but an
**indefinite or concessive construction presupposes a definite
concealed answer** — *"whatever she is,"* *"what he really is,"*
*"something she wasn't telling,"* *"the thing about her that didn't add
up."* *"Whatever she is, she would not pretend otherwise"* asserts
nothing overtly, yet *"whatever she* ***is****"* presupposes the
proposition *she is [some specific concealed thing]* and leaves only
its value blank — surfacing the secret's existence and factivity
exactly as the withholding form does, and echoing the very shape of a
DM note (*what she is*). This is the sub-form most likely to ride in
under **POV-suspicion cover.** Two propositions sit a hair apart: the
PC's **open suspicion** (P1 — *"she's hiding something," "she knows
more than she should"* — grounded in what he observed) is his and is
licensed; a construction that **presupposes a definite withheld answer
exists** (P2 — *"whatever she is"*) is the narrator's certification of
hidden state and is not. Render P1; never P2. The fix keeps the PC's
grounded observations — the seam he sees, the thing she recognized that
she had no business recognizing — and drops the existential: *"she
would not pretend you'd imagined the seam in her."*

### The structural pattern

**Either:** narration includes a negation that names a category the
POV has no access to (Variant A); **or** narration remarks on the
character's non-disclosure of a fact whose withheld clause states,
as true, hidden state the POV has no access to (Variant B); **or** a
speaker (in dialogue or POV direct thought) hedges an *I suspect* line
whose **content** names a specific member of an unnarrowed candidate
set, where the only thing selecting that member is the drafter's
knowledge of the hidden answer (Variant C). In all three, hidden state
— about another character, a setting fact, an upcoming reveal, or the
operative mechanism behind an observed event — that the fiction holds
private has been surfaced into the POV's (and the player's) view by an
emission whose form looks legitimate.

Trigger patterns to scan:

- **Variant A (denial):** *"there is no/nothing X,"* *"no hint of
  X,"* *"no trace of X,"* *"not the X one might expect,"* *"not a
  Y,"* *"no sign of Z,"* *"none of the [tells/marks/smells/sounds]
  of W,"* *"and it is not the [thing it would be if it were the
  hidden thing],"* — any construction where the narrator names a
  category in order to disclaim it.
- **Variant A, emphatic-affirmation sub-form (positive polarity):**
  *"perfectly ordinary," "entirely normal," "just an ordinary
  [day/town/man]," "nothing to worry about," "nothing out of place,"
  "safe as houses,"* — an *intensified* assertion of normality, safety,
  or innocence by a narrator holding hidden contrary state. The
  insistence is what surfaces the contestability; plain, un-intensified
  description does not fire.
- **Variant B (withholding):** *"does not explain how/where/why/what
  X,"* *"leaves X unsaid,"* *"without explaining X,"* *"declines to
  elaborate on X,"* *"offers no account of X,"* *"does not say what
  it cost / how she knew / where he learned"* — flagged only when
  the embedded clause **X** states hidden state as fact.
- **Variant B, presuppositional sub-form (no withholding verb):**
  *"whatever she is,"* *"what he really is,"* *"something she wasn't
  telling,"* *"the thing about her that didn't add up"* —
  indefinite/concessive constructions that presuppose a *definite
  concealed answer exists* about a character carrying hidden state the
  POV cannot confirm. Render the PC's open suspicion (P1), not the
  presupposed withheld answer (P2).
- **Variant C (hypothesis-as-inference):** *"I suspect [specific
  mechanism],"* *"I believe [specific identity],"* *"it may be that
  [specific agenda],"* *"what they took was [specific category]"* — an
  inference-register hedge whose **content** is a specific candidate
  drawn from a wide space of plausible answers the speaker's
  observations do not narrow. The form is the inference register; the
  content is the leak. Distinguished from a legitimate inference by the
  **selectivity test** (below): can the speaker's observations select
  this candidate over its peers? If not, naming it floats the candidate
  into the player's view as the operative hypothesis — and when it
  matches the DM mechanism, the player has been told the DM mechanism.

### How to catch

**Variant A — for each negation in narration (or POV direct
thought), ask:**

**Is this denying something the POV would not otherwise be reading
into the description?**

If yes, the denial is the leak. The POV is not braced against the
category; nothing in the scene was prompting the comparison; the
only reason the category appears in the player's awareness is that
the narrator put it there in order to deny it.

The fix: **describe the thing positively and stop.** The amulet is
silver, has a crescent, is well-made — full stop. The room smells
of hearth and tea — full stop. He answered evenly — full stop. No
*"nothing about it announces…"*; no *"no trace of…"*; no *"none of
the marks of…"* unless those marks have already been established
as something the POV is looking for in this scene.

**Variant A, emphatic-affirmation — for each intensified claim of
normality, safety, or innocence (*perfectly ordinary, entirely normal,
nothing to worry about*), ask: is the narrator reassuring the reader
against a suspicion the POV has not formed?** If yes, the insistence is
the leak. Drop the intensifier and let plain detail carry it: *"The
morning was perfectly ordinary"* → *"The road ran east through wet
bracken; he had directions and a destination."* Show the calm; do not
certify it. (If the POV genuinely braced for a threat that then did not
materialize, the relief is grounded — the same prior-reason test as the
denial exception.)

**Variant B — for each narrated non-disclosure (*does not explain /
leaves unsaid / declines to elaborate / offers no account of*),
inspect the *object* of the withholding:**

**Is it stated as a fact the POV could not independently confirm?**

If yes, the line certifies hidden state. The fix: **rewrite to the
act of withholding only.** The character declines; the player may
infer; the narrator asserts nothing about the concealed content.
*"She does not explain how she came to know the price of a gift from
something larger than herself"* → *"She does not elaborate, and does
not invite the question."* Her own dialogue may stand (she chose to
say what she said, in-fiction); what goes is the narrator's
certification that a specific lived history sits behind it.

### The legitimate exceptions

**Variant A — legitimate negation.** A legitimate negation —
*"the kitchen smelled of bread, not of the copper she expected
after the morning's slaughter"* — is one where **the POV character
has prior reason to be checking for the denied category.** The
morning's slaughter is on the POV's mind; the denial is the POV's
own absence-of-tell registering on her. That is allowed and often
necessary — good narration runs on the register of *the expected
thing that did not appear.*

The illegitimate version is the one where the POV has *no such
prior reason*, and the category appears only because the narrator
brought it in to wave it away. The test is not whether the
sentence contains a negation — most narration legitimately does.
The test is whether the negated category was already on the POV's
shelf before the narrator named it.

**Variant B — legitimate withholding.** Narrating non-disclosure at
the level of **observable behavior** is allowed — *"she did not
elaborate," "he changed the subject," "she let it drop," "she
turned back to her tea"* — because these report only what the POV
sees. The line crosses into leak when the withheld clause asserts
the **specific hidden content** (*"how she came to know the
price"*) rather than merely the act of withholding. The test:
strip the sentence to its withholding verb and ask whether anything
factive about the concealed content survives in the object. If
*"does not explain X"* still tells the player a true thing about X
that they could not otherwise confirm, X is the leak; cut it back
to the bare act.

### Variant C — hypothesis-as-inference (the selectivity rule)

The third surface of the boundary leak. Variants A and B fire on
narration shapes (negation, narrated withholding) that surface hidden
state by remarking on it. Variant C fires on **dialogue** and **POV
direct thought** — a character speaks an *"I suspect / I believe / it
may be that"* line whose form is the legitimate inference register but
whose content names the actual hidden mechanism.

This is the leak shape most likely to ride in under the existing
*"Inference register, or cut"* rewrite move. The hedge satisfies the
form. The content does not. Variants A and B address how hidden state
surfaces in **narrator** voice; Variant C addresses how it surfaces in
**speaker** voice when the speaker is, by character design, supposed to
be guessing.

#### The principle: inference must be selectivity-constrained

A speaker may infer what their observations **select for**. They may not
float a candidate their observations leave **underdetermined**.

The selectivity test is one question: *from the speaker's observations,
how many plausible candidates fit?* If the observations select **one**
candidate — or narrow the field to a small set the speaker honestly
weighs — naming it is grounded inference. If the observations are
consistent with **many** candidates and the speaker names one, the line
has picked a member of an unnarrowed set, and the only thing that
selected it was the drafter's knowledge of which candidate is the DM
answer. The hedge does not unwrite that selection. The player has now
seen the candidate.

A useful frame: distinguish **category-level** facts from
**member-level** facts. The category-level fact is what the speaker's
observations actually constrain — *something happened, of a kind, with
a hand behind it, that touched these people and not those.* The
member-level fact is which specific member of the category was the
mechanism — *memory was the axis, the mechanism is amnesia, the working
is X.* Category-level inference is the speaker's. Member-level
inference, when the observations don't narrow the membership, is the
drafter's, dressed in the speaker's hedge.

#### The canonical slip

Three travelers in an unknown duchy wake to a morning none of them sat
through. They have no record of falling asleep, no person to blame, no
sign of theft. One of them is a power-fluent speaker with grim
familiarity with what workings cost. She is asked what shape this is.

She has evidence for the **category**: *something was taken from those
without amulets that was not taken from us; a working sits on this
duchy; the duchy is built around it.* Her evidence does **not** select
between the many things a working could take — memory, body, position,
life, identity, time, agency, continuity. They are all underdetermined
candidates.

The leak is the line *"I suspect what they took from the hostler and
the boy is memory of the day before — continuity of selves."* The form
is inference register (*I suspect*). The content names the actual DM
mechanism (the cycle's Modify Memory step). The hedge — *I will not put
more weight on this than I can carry* — does not immunize the line: the
hypothesis has already entered the player's view as the operative
candidate, and the player will reason from it whether the speaker
disclaims it or not.

The fix is to **render only the category**: *"Something was taken from
them that was not taken from us — what, I do not know."* The speaker
can name the **shape** her observations select for (a working, a
regime, a condition) and the **category** of what occurred (something
was taken; we were shielded; the duchy is built around it). She cannot
name the **member** of the category — the specific axis of taking —
without overreaching her observations.

#### How to catch it

At the **drafting step**, treat an inference-register hedge like a
reached-for callback: before writing the candidate in, run the
selectivity test on it. *From what this speaker has observed, how many
plausible candidates would fit this slot?* If the answer is *one* (or a
small set the speaker can honestly weigh), write the candidate. If the
answer is *many*, drop down a level of abstraction — name the category
the observations select for, not the member they don't.

At the **gate**, scan every *I suspect / I believe / it may be that*
line in NPC dialogue and POV direct thought for a **specific candidate
in the object position**. For each:

- What observations does this speaker have for selecting *this*
  candidate over its peers?
- If the observations are equally consistent with several plausible
  candidates, the line has picked one. Cut to the category.
- Hedging language (*I suspect, I will not put weight on this*) is not
  a license — it is the cover the leak rides under. The cover does not
  change the content.

A useful sub-test for the borderline case: ask *which candidates the
player would now be reasoning about, after this line.* If the answer
is "the one the speaker named," the line has selected for them. If the
answer is "the category the line described, with several candidates
still open," the line has held the boundary.

#### The legitimate version

Inference is not banned. A speaker can validly say *"there is a working
on this duchy"* (category-level, evidence-selected). A speaker can
validly say *"I have lived near a power that does not negotiate; the
lay of one is recognizable; this is that kind of thing"* (category, not
member). A speaker can validly say *"I do not know what was taken, but
something was, and the amulet is the reason we are not also taken"*
(boundary held to what the observations select for).

The line crosses into Variant C when the speaker descends from the
category to a specific member that the observations don't pick out.
The discipline is not against speculation — it is against speculation
that has been ghost-written by knowledge the speaker doesn't have.

#### Why this slip is hard to catch

Variants A and B fire on **surface forms** the drafter can scan for
(negations, narrated-withholding verbs, presuppositional
*whatever-she-is* constructions). Variant C has no such surface form —
*"I suspect X"* is a grammatical pattern that licenses honest
inference everywhere except when X is the leak. The check is
**semantic, not syntactic.** The drafter must ask, of the specific X
named, whether the speaker's observations select it — and the drafter
is exactly the party who knows the answer and is therefore exactly the
party most tempted to write the X that fits the answer they already
know. The discipline is to hold the speaker to *what their evidence
selects for*, not *what the drafter knows is true*.

### Scope and cost

The check fires on three surfaces: negations (Variant A) and narrated
non-disclosures (Variant B), both in narration and POV-direct-thought;
and hedged hypotheses (Variant C) in dialogue and POV-direct-thought.
Most instances of all three are legitimate — denying categories the
POV is actively considering, registering an expected-but-absent tell,
reporting that a character declined to elaborate, voicing a
candidate-selecting inference. The check is specifically for the
subset where the drafter's hidden knowledge is the only source of the
category being denied (A), the only thing certifying the withheld
content is real (B), or the only thing selecting the named candidate
from a wide plausible set (C).

When the check fires, the rewrite is short: cut the denying clause
(A), strip the withholding to its bare observable act (B), or drop the
named candidate to the category the speaker's observations actually
select for (C). The positive description, the act of declining, or the
honest category-level inference that the leak was attached to still
stands.

**This check also fires on a player-scoped OOC emission — a recap, a
resume refresher, or the DM's answer to a `<…>` player-scoped OOC
query.** Such a block is OOC author-to-player and is therefore tempting
to treat as exempt — but it is bounded by the POV character's
knowledge, and the negated-hidden-state pattern is the exact way it
leaks: *"(player-side: you don't know what she is)"*, *"you're not yet
aware she carries a writ."* Each names a true hidden thing only to deny
the PC's knowledge of it, surfacing it into the player's head — Variant
A applied to a recap. The same slip rides into a *mechanics answer*:
*"magic works here, nothing's suppressing the casting yet"* denies a
hidden world-rule the PC has no concept of, planting it — and the player
asked only what *their own* spells could do (allowed player-meta), not
what the world conceals. The fix is the same: cut the reference entirely;
describe only what the PC has observed or legitimately tracks. See the player-scoped OOC carve-out in
[epistemic_discipline_checklist.md]({{PLUGIN_ROOT}}/engines/prose-engine/scene/references/epistemic_discipline_checklist.md)
and the RESUME section of [scene_lifecycle.md]({{PLUGIN_ROOT}}/engines/prose-engine/scene/references/scene_lifecycle.md).

The fact may still be true in the fiction (the amulet *is* not, in
any way the world recognizes, vampire-coded; she *did* learn the
price the hard way). The line is wrong because the *category* (A) or
the *certified hidden content* (B) was surfaced to a POV with no
access to it, and the surfacing put it in the player's head.

## The fabricated-quantity failure mode — ungrounded counts, measures, and calculations surfacing as fact

A thirteenth slip, content-based and the quantitative sibling of the
positive grounding trace. Where that check asks whether an NPC's claim
has an in-fiction event grounding it, this one asks a narrower question
of a specific kind of claim: **does this number trace to grounded source
data?** It fires in narration as readily as in dialogue, and it is the
slip most likely to ride into a player-facing recap or resume refresher,
where the urge to sound concrete and oriented is strongest.

The trigger is **any asserted quantity**: a count (*a dozen vials, three
bodies, forty guardsmen*), a tally or sum (*two hundred crowns, half the
harvest*), an elapsed time or relative-time interval (*three days back,
six weeks ago, a year before the siege*), a distance (*a half-mile off,
two ridgelines over*), an age, a duration, or any other number —
including the vague-quantifier forms that still commit to a magnitude
(*dozens, hundreds, a handful, for weeks*). It also includes
**clock-position references in dialogue** — phrasings that place the
scene at a point on its *own* clock rather than naming an interval
between two events: absolute marks (*by hour seven, just an hour in,
hours yet*) and relative or fractional ones (*past midway, near the
end, a comfortable margin past the middle, about there*).

The hazard is **slot-driven reaching** (see the pre-write reference
check): a sentence-frame wants specificity, a number is within reach, and
the drafter supplies one that no source actually grounds. The fabricated
value reads as authoritative precisely *because* it is precise — the
reader has no way to tell an invented "three days" from a sourced one.

### The clock-position sub-case

The relative and fractional clock-marks are the ones most apt to ride
past the gate, because they wear the costume of mood rather than
measurement. *We're past the middle by a comfortable margin* or *we're
near the end* reads as a character's felt sense of a long stretch, not as
a claim about the scene clock — yet each commits to a midpoint or a
fraction, and that position must trace to the saved period stamps like
any other number. The pull is the *clock-mark feeling* — we're well into
this, we're nearly through — reaching for a position without checking it.

A line lands here **even when the underlying elapse was stamped
correctly.** Getting the clock honest upstream is the job of
`{{PLUGIN_ROOT}}/engines/prose-engine/scene/references/time_and_events.md` (its undeclared-elapse rule keeps register and
prose-length from inflating the elapse); this check is the downstream
gate that catches a *dialogue* line asserting a mark the stamp
contradicts. The canonical pair: a sentry on a long night watch says
*"I'd have guessed I'd be fighting to stay awake by the seventh hour —
and here we are,"* when the saved stamps put the watch at its third
hour; and, earlier, *"we're past the midpoint of the watch by a
comfortable margin"* at a point that, against the saved dawn-relief
time, is still only *approaching* the midpoint. The fatigue is real;
the position asserted is not.

The same sub-case has a **narration-side twin** that is even easier to
miss, because it carries no clock-*mark* at all — only a part-of-day
label or a light-state. *A grey slat of afternoon,* *the light was
going,* *daylight he couldn't spare,* *get under a roof before dark* each
commit the scene to a position on its own clock as firmly as *hour seven*
does, while reading as pure setting-atmosphere. The strongest pull here
is **carry-over**: a previous scene ended in the late afternoon under a
race-the-dark pressure, and the next scene — set in a fresh morning —
inherits that time-of-day and that pressure by reflex, because the
*feeling* of the prior scene is still in hand while its clock is not. The
fix is the same: re-derive the scene's time-of-day from its own grounded
time anchor before rendering any part-of-day label or light cue, and
never let a prior scene's clock or its attendant urgency ride into a new
one. When two scenes sit on different days, or different parts of a day,
the ambient time is a coordinate to fetch, not a mood to reuse.

The fix for this sub-case has a third route beyond loosen-or-cut: when a
line wants to convey *we're at the fatigue mark / approaching the end /
past the middle*, either trace the real position from the saved stamps
and render the correct mark, or drop the number and render the **felt
quality only** (*we're at the point where I'd usually be tired*) without
committing to a position the data contradicts.

### What grounding requires — and what it does not

Grounding does **not** require reproducing the source's exact value. A
source that establishes three hundred dead may be rendered as *three
hundred*, *a few hundred*, *hundreds*, or *the better part of a thousand*
— any phrasing whose magnitude is consistent with the grounded value is
fine, and looser phrasing is often the better prose. What grounding
forbids is:

- **Invented precision.** Supplying a specific number when the source
  gives none. If the files say the party "traveled the east road into the
  foothills" with no duration attached, *three days back* is fabricated;
  *recently* or *on the road in* is grounded.
- **Contradiction.** Rendering a value the source rules out. If the event
  log places the pitch and the arrival on the same day, *three days back*
  is not merely unsourced — it contradicts the timeline.

### The fix

- **If a grounded value exists,** render it at whatever precision the line
  needs, consistent with the source. Loosen freely; do not tighten past
  what the source supports.
- **If no grounded value exists,** either use a non-committal quantifier
  that commits to no magnitude (*some, several, a while, not long*) or
  drop the quantity entirely and let the sentence stand without a number.
  A line without a count is cleaner than a line with an invented one.

### How to catch it

At the **drafting step**, treat a reached-for number like a reached-for
callback: before writing it in, name the source that supplies it. At the
**gate**, scan the drafted output (narration, dialogue, and any recap or
refresher) for every quantity and ask the same question — *what grounds
this number?* If nothing does, loosen it to a vague quantifier or cut it.

**This check also fires on a player-facing recap or resume refresher.**
The refresher is bounded by the POV character's knowledge for its
*content*; it is bounded by the source data for its *quantities*. The
"three days back" slip is the canonical example: an OOC orientation block
reaching for a concrete interval the files never supplied.

The fact may be true in the fiction (something may indeed have a count).
The line is wrong because the *specific quantity asserted* was invented or
contradicts the grounded value, and the precision made the invention read
as fact.

## The spatiotemporal-binding failure mode — a true event recalled at a false time or place

A fourteenth slip, and the third member of the grounding family (with the
positive grounding trace and the fabricated-quantity check). Where the
positive grounding trace asks *whether* a fact is known to the speaker, and
the quantity check asks whether a *number* traces to source data, this one
asks a question neither covers: when the draft **recalls an established
event** and pins a **time or place** onto it, *does that binding match saved
state?*

The hazard is specific and it is sneaky precisely because the recalled fact
is **real**. The drafter reaches not for an invented event but for a *true*
one already in working memory — and, reaching for rhetorical contrast or a
sense of orientation, welds an explicit *when* or *where* onto it that the
source does not support. The canonical instance: a companion's warning that
in fact occurred *at the campfire, minutes ago, in the current conversation*
was recalled in narration as having happened *"two hours and a hard road
ago"* — out on the journey, before arrival. The event was grounded. The
clock value was a coherent, ordinary interval. The **binding** —
event-occurred-at-that-time — was false, and it contradicted the event log.

### Why the other grounding checks miss it

- The **positive grounding trace** clears it: the event *is* grounded; the
  speaker/narrator does have it. The trace asks "is this known?" — answer
  yes — and passes on.
- The **quantity check** catches only the *number*. Strip the number and the
  bug survives intact: *"a while ago, out on the road"* asserts the same
  false binding with no quantity to flag.

The leak rides in on the recalled fact's credibility. Because the event is
true and salient, the composite claim inherits its grounded status, and the
time/place coordinate is treated as belonging to an already-checked unit —
decoration on a grounded fact rather than a fresh factual assertion of its
own. It is not decoration. *A placement of an event on the timeline (or map)
is a claim equal in grounding obligation to the event itself.*

### The (E, P, T) tuple — what "grounded" means here

Model the recalled claim as a tuple of an event **E**, a place **P**, and a
time **T**. Any two or more bound together (E-at-T, E-at-P, P-at-T) form a
**binding**. The trigger is an explicit **T** or **P** pinned onto recalled
content. The check is **on the binding, not the slots**:

- A **missing** E or P is vacuously grounded — there is nothing to falsify.
  (P-at-T with no event — "we reached the hut at full dusk" — is a perfectly
  groundable, often true, binding.)
- Grounding the slots *independently* is **not sufficient** and is the exact
  trap: in the canonical bug every slot was individually fine. "Grounded"
  means the **asserted binding matches saved state** — you fetch the event's
  (or place's) real coordinates from the log/timeline/scene record and
  confirm the asserted T (and P) coincide.

### Backward recall only — forward claims are the future-state check

This check fires on **recall of established / prior** state. Forward
authorship that *sets new* story-state ("an hour later, the rain stopped")
cannot contradict saved state — it is additive — and a *speaker* asserting a
future event-at-a-time is already governed by the future-state failure mode.
Scoping to backward recall keeps the two from double-covering and avoids
demanding a lookup against nothing.

### How to catch it

At the **drafting step**, treat an explicit *when*/*where* pinned to a
recalled event like a reached-for number or callback: before writing it in,
name the source that fixes that event's actual time and place. At the
**gate**, for every recalled event carrying an explicit T or P, fetch its
saved coordinates and confirm the binding. If the asserted time/place
contradicts the source, correct it to the grounded coordinate, or drop the
explicit coordinate for a relative phrase that stays true (*"minutes ago, by
this same fire"*). Fires on narration, dialogue, and player-facing
recaps/refreshers alike — the orientation block, reaching to sound situated,
is a prime host.

The fact may be true in the fiction (the event really happened). The line is
wrong because the *when* or *where* bound to it was not the event's, and the
real event's credibility made the misplacement read as fact.

## The fabricated-concrete failure mode — narration conjures an ungrounded who/what/where to fill a rhetorical slot

A grounding-family slip, narration-side, and the third product of
**slot-driven reaching** (with the misfit callback and the fabricated
quantity; see the pre-write reference check). Where the fabricated-quantity
check guards an invented *number* and the spatiotemporal-binding check guards
a *recalled event* welded to a false time or place, this one guards against
the narrator **conjuring an entity, a presence, a location, or a piece of
someone's knowledge** that no source grounds — and slipping it into the POV
character's knowledge-horizon — because a sentence-frame demanded a filler.

The root cause is the same as the misfit callback: a frame has a shape that
wants completing, and the drafter supplies a concrete by reflex. The frames
that most often demand it are **parallel lists and tricolons** (*"a duchy
that owed back-taxes, and a woman who knew you were coming, and whatever a
goddess wanted in a place like this"*), **"what lies ahead / beyond /
waiting" gestures**, and **motif echoes** — a theme established earlier
(*someone had been expecting them*) exerting gravity on a later sentence
until it materializes a someone to satisfy the motif. The pull is the
motif's, not the POV's perception.

The leak is sharpest in **POV-anchored narration**, where the conjured
concrete is framed as part of *what the POV is oriented by* — what they are
walking toward, who they know awaits, what the place ahead holds. The POV
has no such entity in their grounded knowledge. The canonical instance: a
tricolon naming what lies past a border lands *"a woman who knew you were
coming"* in its middle slot — but the only person who knew the POV was
coming is behind them, outside the border. The line either **misplaces** that
real person ahead, or **conjures** a second, inside watcher the POV has no
basis to believe in. Either way the narrator has furnished the POV's horizon
from the author's knowledge (or from nothing at all), to complete a rhythm.

### Why the other grounding checks miss it

- The **positive grounding trace** is scoped to NPC claims and implications;
  a narrator's establishing sentence is not its primary surface.
- The **fabricated-quantity** check catches only numbers; strip any number
  and the conjured *entity* survives intact.
- The **spatiotemporal-binding** check fires on *backward recall* of an
  established event; this is a *forward* gesture conjuring a *new* presence,
  not a misbinding of a logged one.
- **Off-screen-presence** catches an *ambient sensory detail* (a sound, a
  light, a tread) attributed to a *named or silhouetted specific* character;
  here there is no sensory detail and often no named character — just an
  indefinite (*a woman*) asserted into the POV's horizon.

### The discriminator — fabrication vs. legitimate forward worldbuilding

The narrator may freely introduce *new* world content ahead of the party —
that is additive authorship, not a leak. The line crosses into leak when the
conjured concrete is asserted as part of the **POV's epistemic situation**:
something the POV knows awaits, is oriented by, or is knowingly walking
toward. The test: strip the concrete to what the POV has actually been
shown. If the POV could not name this who/what/where from their own grounded
knowledge, and the sentence presents it as part of *their* horizon, it is
conjured — cut it or demote it to the genuine unknown.

### How to catch it

At the **drafting step**, treat a who/what/where reached for to complete a
parallel list, a "what lies ahead" gesture, or a motif echo exactly like a
reached-for callback: before writing it in, name the source that grounds
*that entity, in that place, as known to the POV.* At the **gate**, for each
concrete the narration places in the POV's knowledge-horizon, confirm the
POV can ground it. If not, rewrite to one of:

- **The genuine unknown.** Render what lies ahead as unknown to the POV —
  *"whatever a hermit on the edge of the world thought worth arming three
  strangers against"* — rather than as a furnished concrete.
- **Relocate to the grounded position.** If the concrete is a real entity in
  the wrong place (the woman who knew is *behind* the POV, not ahead), put it
  where the POV actually grounds it.
- **Drop the slot.** A two-member list, or a sentence without the tricolon's
  third beat, is cleaner than one with a conjured member forced in. The motif
  does not need a body to land.

The entity may be real, or may become real later in the fiction. The line is
wrong because the narrator placed it in the POV's knowledge-horizon to
satisfy a frame, with nothing grounding the POV's knowledge of it.

### Retrieved and derived concretes — a consulted source yields only what it holds

The fabricated-concrete slip has a second surface that looks, at draft time,
like the *opposite* of fabrication: the concrete is not conjured to fill a
rhythm — it is **retrieved.** The POV deliberately consults an in-world
information source — an archive of notes, a ledger of accounts, a map, a set
of records, a divination, or a character's own memory — and the beat reports
what the consultation "turns up." The retrieval framing *feels* grounded (the
character **looked it up**), and a **skill-check success** amplifies the
feeling: the drafter reads "she rolled well on searching the archive" as a
licence to produce a rich, specific answer.

It is not. A consulted source is grounded to exactly **what that source is
documented to contain** — and no skill total converts an absence into a fact.
Two failure shapes:

1. **The source is made to hold what it does not.** The source, per its own
   established contents (or its author's documented knowledge-boundary), does
   not contain the retrieved fact — yet the beat has it yield one. The trap is
   sharpest when the source's keeper has *already said* the source lacks it: a
   keeper who has told the consulting character *"my notes never fixed the
   place — I know it only by the gap"* cannot then have those notes fix the
   place because the reader searched them well. A source cannot output what its
   author never put in it; a research success on such a source returns the
   **documented absence**, framed as the finding — *"scrap after scrap circles
   the place and never once fixes it; the author truly never knew where it
   was."* The gap **is** the honest result.

2. **The retrieved fact contradicts canon.** Retrieved and derived content is
   **canon-gated exactly like narration and dialogue.** A detail surfaced from
   a ledger, a map, or a divination that conflicts with documented world-state
   is a leak with a longer fuse than a spoken one: the POV now *acts* on false
   intelligence — is sent to the wrong place, spends real effort chasing what
   was never there — and the contradiction surfaces (if ever) only after the
   cost is paid. A skill-check success that produces canon-violating output is
   the worst case, because the success *dignifies* the false fact as hard-won.

**What a check-success legitimately buys.** A high roll on consulting a source
is real and worth playing — it operates on the **holdings**, not beyond them.
A strong result lets the POV **surface** what the source contains, **collate**
scattered entries into a pattern their author never assembled, **sharpen** a
vague holding to the precision the source actually supports, and **notice** a
connection between two real records. It never **originates** a holding the
source lacks. The line: a check moves what is *in* the source into the
character's hands more completely; it does not add to the source.

**The drafting test.** Before a consultation "turns up" a concrete, name where
in the source's *documented contents* that concrete lives, and confirm it does
not contradict canon. If the source is documented not to hold it — or its
keeper has said as much — the beat returns the absence. If producing the
concrete requires the source to know something it canonically cannot, it is
fabrication wearing a retrieval's coat, and the check that "found" it only
makes the misdirection more expensive.

**A character's own recorded suspicion is such a source — and it holds only
the suspicion.** A POV's saved belief-state — a working hypothesis in their
memory block, an *inference* logged in their overlay (*the master is not
home,* *she is hiding something,* *the place is watched*) — is an in-world
source exactly like an archive or a memory: consulted at draft time, it
yields **that the POV suspects the proposition,** never that the proposition
is true. It is not a canon source and must not be promoted to one — neither
narrated as established fact (a character-aligned narrator asserting the
suspected proposition as world-truth) nor handed to an NPC to *confirm* with
concrete detail (the info-source made to corroborate the guess). The pull is
strong precisely because the belief is the POV's *own* and sits in
working-memory beside the fiction, indistinguishable at a glance from a fact
they perceived. Before the narrator or any character treats a POV's inference
as true, verify the proposition against setting canon — the location and its
documented residents, the named authorities who actually hold the ground. The
POV keeps their suspicion; the world keeps whatever it actually is, which the
canon and not the guess decides. (The inverse cost is the sharpest form of
this: the suspicion is not merely unproven but *false* against canon, and the
narrator has just certified the POV's error as the world's truth.)

## The confirmation-mirror failure mode — an NPC's testimony authored toward the POV's private hypothesis

The most structural of the boundary slips, because it is not a token that
appears in the draft but a **direction of authorship** that produces the
draft. It fires when the POV holds a private suspicion — a theory voiced
only OOC, an inference logged in an overlay, or merely a reading live in the
drafter's working memory — and an NPC is then queried on that topic. The
correct computation runs one way: the NPC's answer is derived from the NPC's
**own grounded record,** blind to the suspicion. The leak runs it the other
way — the drafter, knowing the party's theory, authors the NPC's line
*toward* it: a testimony shaped to confirm, resonate with, or eerily satisfy
the private read, so the NPC becomes a **mirror** reflecting the party's
hypothesis back as though it were independent corroboration.

The corruption is double. First, it is a **knowledge-boundary breach**
(row 1): the NPC has no access to the party's unspoken suspicion — nothing
in what was said to them carries it — yet their answer is built around it.
Second, and worse, it **manufactures false independent confirmation.** A
party's suspicion gains weight when a source they did not prompt turns out to
share it — but that weight is real evidence *only if the source arrived there
on its own.* An NPC steered to agree hands the party counterfeit
corroboration, the most expensive kind of leak: it is *acted on* as if two
independent minds converged, when only one did, doubled back through the
drafter.

The tell is the **convenience of the agreement.** When an NPC's testimony
lands squarely on what the party privately believes, that alignment is a red
flag, not a satisfying beat. Independent sources do not conveniently
corroborate unspoken theories. If an NPC's answer confirms the party's hidden
read, the alignment must trace to the NPC's *own grounded record* — and if it
does not, the answer was reverse-engineered from the theory and is
fabricated.

**Grounded knowledge wins — especially when it refutes the theory.** The NPC
speaks what their record holds. When their grounded knowledge diverges from
the party's suspicion, the divergence *is* the point: an NPC who states what
they know and *contradicts* the party's read is the sign the flow is right. A
town's own historian, asked about a ruler the party privately believes
absent, answers from his documentation — names the sitting ruler, gives the
house's recent history — and in doing so refutes the guess. That refutation
is not the NPC being unhelpful; it is the world being *independent of the
party's beliefs,* which is the only condition under which any later
confirmation from it would mean anything.

**How to catch it.** At the drafting step, before writing an NPC's answer on
a topic the POV has a private theory about, compute the answer from the NPC's
record **as if you did not know the theory.** Then check the line you wrote:
does it happen to confirm the party's suspicion? If so, verify that
confirmation is *forced by the NPC's own grounded knowledge* — not supplied
because you knew what would resonate. If the NPC's record is silent, the NPC
says they do not know; if the NPC's record contradicts the suspicion, the NPC
contradicts it. The party's theory is never an input to the NPC's testimony.
The fix is generative, not a post-hoc scan: firewall the NPC's answer to the
NPC's record, exactly as the cross-character record-pull check firewalls
character A's content to A's own record.

## The language-comprehension failure mode — speaker understands content in a language they do not have

A perception-based slip, dormant by default. When speech or writing is
rendered in a specific language or script, only characters who hold that
language (spoken) or script (written) in their known-languages set may
comprehend its **meaning**. A character without it may hear sounds or see
marks, and may even recognize *that* it is a language and perhaps which —
but they do not understand it, translate it, or respond to its content.

The slip is structurally distinct from identity-through-disguise (which
concerns recognizing a person) and from knowledge-attribution (which
concerns interior state). This concerns **decoding a linguistic signal**
the character has no key for.

Examples (generic):

- A character who holds only Language A answers a question that was asked
  in Language B — the line responds to the *content*, so comprehension is
  asserted.
- Narration POV: *"She read the inscription over the door"* — when the POV
  character's literacy set does not include that script.
- A character "translates" overheard talk in a tongue not in their set,
  even loosely, even hedged as a paraphrase.

The default state, in the absence of any language tracking, is full
comprehension — every character understands everything, and this check is
dormant. It activates only when the override supplies a known-languages
field.

### Required parameter — the known-languages field

The engine does not name the field — the consuming game's override defines:

- **The field name(s)** for the spoken-language set and, if literacy is
  tracked separately, the readable-script set.
- **The source** — typically the character sheet, possibly extended by an
  overlay (a script learned in play).
- **The strict default** when the field is absent for a character —
  recommended: comprehension limited to the setting's common tongue only.

If the override does not supply such a field, the check is dormant — the
engine does not synthesize a default.

### How to catch it

When a character comprehends, translates, or responds to the meaning of
language content:

1. Identify the comprehending character A and the content's language /
   script L.
2. Read A's known-languages / literacy set.
3. If L is not in A's set, rewrite: render the content as unintelligible
   to A (sound or marks only), let A recognize *that* it is a language
   without its meaning, route comprehension through a literate party
   member who can translate, or cut the comprehension.

The character may legitimately register *that* a thing was said or written
— and react to tone, volume, or the fact of being addressed — but cannot
act on the meaning.

### Scope and cost

The check fires only when language content carrying meaning is present and
a character engages with that meaning. The lookup is one field read per
comprehending character. In monolingual scenes where every participant
shares the rendered language, the check short-circuits immediately.

The content may be perfectly real in the fiction. The line is wrong
because the character has no key to decode it.

## The disposition-surfacing failure mode — speaker displays a relationship they have no grounds to know

A knowledge-boundary slip, kin to the basic boundary check (rows 1 & 3 of
the gate). A character's knowledge of the **relationships among others** —
who serves whom, who is allied or estranged, who outranks whom, the
standing between two third parties — is itself bounded. A character may
only display a relationship-fact their own block grounds. Treating a
concealed ally per their *true* allegiance, or naming a tie between two
other characters, leaks a relationship the speaker was never shown.

This is the social-graph analog of the basic boundary: the basic check
guards facts and events; this guards the **edges between characters**.

Examples (generic):

- A character addresses a stranger as the agent of a faction the stranger
  has never disclosed working for.
- *"Her handler had sent her"* in POV narration filtered through a
  character who has no way of knowing the second figure is a handler at
  all.
- A character brokers between two others on the assumption they are rivals,
  when that rivalry is not in the speaker's block.

### The structural pattern

The pull is the same as any boundary slip: the *author* knows the
relationship map, and the map quietly furnishes a character's behavior. The
character acts correctly with respect to the true graph rather than with
respect to the slice of the graph they have actually observed.

### How to catch it

When a character's line or behavior turns on a relationship, allegiance, or
standing among others:

1. Identify the displaying character A and the relationship R being acted
   on.
2. Check A's relationships / disposition block (including their *read of*
   the parties involved) for R.
3. If R is not grounded there, rewrite to an observable-behavior or
   grounded-suspicion register — *"the two of them moved like they'd done
   this before"* — and drop the attributed allegiance, or cut.

The character may legitimately *suspect* a tie from observed behavior, but
cannot assert or rely on it as known.

### Scope and cost

The check fires only when a line or action depends on a relationship among
others. One block read per displaying character; in scenes where every
relevant tie is already in the character's block, it short-circuits.

The relationship may be real. The line is wrong because the speaker was
never shown it.

## The agenda-surfacing failure mode — speaker acts on or asserts another character's hidden plan

A knowledge-boundary slip, kin to the basic boundary check (rows 1 & 3) and,
for forward-framed cases, to the future-state check (row 7). A character's
**goals, intentions, and hidden plans** are boundary state: other characters
must not act on them, assert them as fact, or frame them as inevitable
without an in-fiction route to that knowledge.

This is the intention analog of the basic boundary: it guards what a
character privately *means to do*, as distinct from what they have said or
done observably.

Examples (generic):

- A character pre-empts another's secret plan — moving to block it — when
  that plan has never been spoken or observably signaled.
- *"He'll go for the ledger first"* asserted of another character's
  undisclosed intention as settled fact (also a future-state slip).
- A character explains a third party's motive to a fourth as if it were
  known, when it lives only in the third party's agenda block.

### The structural pattern

Again the author's omniscience furnishes the behavior: because the plan is
known to the author, a character behaves as though forewarned. The tell is
a character being *correct about an intention they were never let in on*.

### How to catch it

When a character acts on, asserts, or forward-frames another character's
intention:

1. Identify the speaker A and the intention I attributed to character B.
2. Check whether I is grounded in A's block — disclosed by B, observably
   signaled, or relayed through a documented source.
3. If not, recast as A's own inference / projection (*"I'd guess he wants
   the ledger"*), or cut. For forward *"they will do X"* framing, apply the
   future-state rewrite: drop to conditional or inferential.

The character may project, guess, or read body language — but cannot treat
another's private agenda as established.

### Scope and cost

The check fires only when a line or action turns on another character's
intention. One block read per attributed character; intentions the speaker
genuinely witnessed being disclosed short-circuit.

The plan may be real. The line is wrong because the speaker was never let
in on it.

## The cross-character record-pull failure mode — drafting one character's content from another's loaded record

A knowledge-boundary slip, kin to the basic boundary check (row 1), the
knowledge-attribution check (row 3), the disposition-surfacing check
(row 17), and the agenda-surfacing check (row 18). All five guard the
same seam from different angles: a character's content (dialogue,
interiority, read-of-another) must trace to **that character's**
resolved record, never to the resolved record of another character whom
the model also has loaded in working memory.

This row exists despite the basic boundary check because the slip has a
**structural failure surface the boundary check does not catch on its
own.** At scene start the model resolves every participant — init,
experience overlays, timeline — and holds those records side-by-side.
When the model then composes one character A's read of another
character B, it has full access to B's record and pulls from it as
easily as from A's. The boundary check at the end of the gate scans
for *leaks at the dialogue layer* — and direct in-fiction questions
(*"What do you do for a living?"*) discriminate cleanly by speech-act
shape, forcing an explicit "does A know this?" at the dialogue layer.
But catalogue beats, *"she could tell that he…"* reads, third-person
reads of bearing, and rich biographical interiority of B's life carry
**no such discriminator in the shape**, and the source pollution at
the working-memory layer slides through unless drafting actively
firewalls A's record from B's. The pull of the omniscience is strongest
when the leaked fact would *naturally* explain the surface A is
reading — a profession that explains a bearing, a history that
explains a reaction. The fact *belongs* in the read, except that the
reader does not have it.

### The structural pattern

The slip has two structural signatures:

- A **frame without a discriminator.** Catalogue beats, *"she could
  tell"* reads, *"his hands told her he had been…"* reads, the
  in-fiction recap one character gives of another. None of these
  shapes carry the explicit "does A know?" check that a direct
  question forces. The frame invites a rich, *grounded-feeling* read
  of B, and the model fills it from whatever B-record is loaded.
- A **fact that naturally explains an observable surface.** The
  bearing of a working professional, the way hands move, the
  composure on the bed, the calmness under restraint. The model
  reaches for the explanation it has — B's profession, B's history,
  B's identity flag — because that explanation makes the surface
  legible. The author-omniscience pressure to fill in the *why* of an
  observed surface, from material the model already has in working
  memory, is what surfaces the leak.

The tell is a character being *correct about a biographical fact they
were never told.* A reader who tracks back from the dialogue to the
record finds the fact in B's resolved record only.

### How to catch it

A per-character source firewall, applied at **draft time**, not only
at gate time:

1. Identify the speaker / POV anchor A and the read target B.
2. For every named factual attribute of B in the draft — profession,
   history, identity flag, named entity in B's life, biographical
   detail — trace it to A's resolved record (init + experience
   overlay + timeline + transcript sweep).
3. If the attribute is in A's record (disclosed to A, observed by A,
   present in a beat A participated in), admissible.
4. If the attribute is only in B's record, **bracket out B's record
   as inadmissible source material for A's content** and recompose
   the line from A's observable surface only. A may name what they
   read (the carriage of a hand, a working register in the voice, a
   way of asking that is plain not pitched); A may infer a **category**
   from those surfaces (*"a man used to nights with stakes,"
   "someone who has done this work long enough to know the
   difference"*); A may **not** name the *specific attribute* the
   surface points to.

The discipline is **positive** — firewall A's content to A's record —
not the negative *check for leaks after drafting*. By the time a
catalogue beat is drafted with the leaked fact in it, the fact has
been integrated into the prose so deeply that catching it requires
unwinding the whole read. Doing the firewall upstream — *what is the
inadmissible source material for this line, and what does that leave
me?* — is the only reliable defense.

### Scope and frequency

The check fires in two specific structural contexts often, and rarely
elsewhere:

- **Catalogue / read-of-another beats.** Any beat in which one
  character takes the floor to read another to them — explicitly
  framed (*"I've been finding…"*) or interior (*"watching her, she
  could tell…"*).
- **Resume refreshers and recap blocks** that include another
  character's biographical context. (The player-scoped OOC carve-out
  already governs the PC's own knowledge bounds in a refresher; this
  firewall extends the same discipline to *every other* character
  the refresher names — a refresher in A's POV may not surface B's
  record-only attributes through narration either.)

The fact may be true in the world. The line is wrong because the
speaker was never let in on it — and the *narration in A's POV* is
the speaker.

## Epistemic-pass discipline — the final gate

The epistemic-pass is the **last thing you do before posting any NPC
response.** Nothing else runs after it. If override rules apply, they
apply at draft time *or* extend the epistemic-pass's scan list — they do
**not** run as a separate later pass that could re-introduce violations
the epistemic-pass has already cleared.

### Order of operations for every response

1. **Draft the response** with all in-fiction rules already applied —
   the rules in this file, plus any in-fiction rules the override file
   defines (e.g., banning project-internal time indices from dialogue,
   substituting a setting-specific calendar, content-rating gradation).
   These are drafting constraints, not post-hoc filters. The
   pre-write reference check (below) applies during this step, every
   time the drafting step reaches for a callback, reference, quotation,
   or analogy.
2. **Run the epistemic-pass as the final gate**, scanning the drafted
   response for:
   - **The epistemic violation check.** Anything the character is saying or
     implying based on knowledge they don't have. If found, revise.
   - **The positive grounding trace.** For each meaningful claim or
     implication in each NPC line (including prepositional phrases,
     deictic gestures, and free relative clauses — *on you, in there,
     that, what you came for, whatever you came carrying*), name the
     in-fiction sensory event — current or remembered — that grounds
     it for *this* NPC. If none exists, rewrite to the inference
     register the NPC is entitled to, or cut the implication. This is
     the gate's defense against the PC-possessed-item failure mode
     above.
   - **The quantity-grounding check.** For each asserted quantity in the
     drafted output — count, tally, sum, elapsed/relative time, distance,
     age, duration, or other number, including vague-but-committing forms
     (*dozens, a handful, for weeks*) — name the source data that grounds
     it. This also covers **clock-position references in dialogue** that
     place the scene at a point on its own clock — absolute (*by hour seven,
     just an hour in, hours yet*) or relative/fractional (*past midway, near
     the end, a comfortable margin past the middle, about there*). The
     relative/fractional forms are the ones most likely to slip the gate:
     they read as mood-setting register rather than a committing quantity,
     yet they still pin a midpoint or fraction that must trace to the saved
     period stamps. (Correctly *stamping* the clock is the upstream job of
     `{{PLUGIN_ROOT}}/engines/prose-engine/scene/references/time_and_events.md`; this check is the gate that catches a line drifting
     from the stamp.) Exact precision is not required: any rendering
     consistent with the grounded value is allowed, and looser phrasing is
     often better. If no source grounds the value, or the rendered value
     contradicts the source, loosen it to a non-committal quantifier (*some,
     several, a while*) — or, for a felt clock-position, render the quality
     without the mark (*at the point where I'd usually be tired*) — or cut
     the number. Fires on narration, dialogue, and player-facing
     recaps/refreshers alike. This is the gate's defense against the
     fabricated-quantity failure mode above.
   - **The spatiotemporal-binding check.** For each *recall* of an
     established/prior event that pins an explicit time or place onto it
     (*"two hours ago," "back at the hut," "the morning we crossed"*), fetch
     that event's saved coordinates and confirm the asserted when/where
     matches. The check is on the **binding**, not the slots — a true event
     plus a coherent clock value can still bind falsely (the warning that
     happened *at the fire* recalled as *two hours back on the road*). A
     missing event or place is vacuously grounded. If the binding contradicts
     the source, correct to the grounded coordinate or drop the explicit
     when/where for a true relative phrase. Forward event-at-time claims are
     the future-state check, not this one. Fires on narration, dialogue, and
     recaps/refreshers alike. This is the gate's defense against the
     spatiotemporal-binding failure mode above.
   - **The fabricated-concrete check.** For each who/what/where the
     narration places in the POV character's knowledge-horizon — an entity,
     a presence, a location, or a piece of someone's knowledge, especially
     one completing a parallel list / tricolon, a *what-lies-ahead* gesture,
     or a motif echo — confirm the POV can ground it from their own
     perception. A concrete the POV could not name from grounded knowledge,
     presented as part of what they are oriented by or knowingly moving
     toward (a person *"who knew you were coming"* conjured ahead when the
     only such person is behind/outside; an implied watcher the POV has no
     basis for), is conjured: rewrite it to the genuine unknown, relocate it
     to the position the POV actually grounds, or drop the slot. Legitimate
     forward worldbuilding — new state not claimed as POV knowledge — is
     exempt. This is the gate's defense against the fabricated-concrete
     failure mode above.
   - **The knowledge-attribution check.** For each verb of cognition or
     perception applied to a character other than the speaker (*knew,
     believed, foresaw, understood, expected, saw, realized, sensed,
     suspected, decided, came in believing*), verify the assertion
     against that character's file at time T. If unsupported, rewrite
     to the speaker's own belief, the inference register, or remove the
     claim. This is the gate's defense against the knowledge-attribution
     failure mode above.
   - **The roster-at-time-T check** *(dormant unless the override
     supplies a join-point field name).* For each group reference (*the
     party, the group, the others, the expedition, the company*),
     verify every implied member's join-point field against scene-time
     T. If any implied member's join-point is later than T, rewrite the
     reference. If an implied PC lacks the join-point field, surface to
     the user rather than guess. This is the gate's defense against the
     roster-at-time-T failure mode above.
   - **The infrastructure-citation scan.** Every narration aside for any
     of: *overlay, file, notes, base, saved, established, marked,
     recorded, per the, as the.* If found in narration (not in
     user-facing meta), rewrite the line to state the in-fiction fact
     without the citation. The fact stays; the source vanishes.
     **Same scan, applied to character dialogue and direct POV:** flag
     any self-description in which the character names a behavior,
     register, or pattern of their own using the author's tracking
     vocabulary — coined register names, behavior-category labels,
     voice-note coinages, recurring-pattern handles that exist only in
     the storage layer's tracking of them. The character has no
     tracking-layer perspective on themselves; they live the register,
     they do not name it with the file's label. Rewrite to
     description-from-inside-experience, demonstration without label,
     or a natural in-character coinage. This is the gate's defense
     against the author-tracking-vocabulary slip above.
   - **The off-screen-presence check.** For each narration reference
     that attributes an off-screen ambient detail (sound, motion, sign
     of habitation, distant figure, light, smell) to a named character
     not present in the scene, verify a loaded source places
     that character at this location at scene-time T. If no source
     places them there, rewrite to environment, an unnamed generic
     role, or a party member — or remove the attribution. Hedged
     attribution (*could be X, might be Y*) fires this check the same
     as firm assertion. This is the gate's defense against the
     off-screen-presence failure mode above.
   - **The future-state check.** For each indicative future-tense or
     temporal-subordinate construction (*will, shall, when X arrives,
     after X happens, by the time X, the minute he reads*), identify
     the referenced future event. If the speaker has documented
     foresight covering that event (per an override-supplied
     foresight field), or the event has been jointly planned in
     shared scene context, the claim is allowed. Otherwise rewrite to
     conditional or inferential register, or cut. Without an
     override-supplied foresight field the check runs in its strict
     default: every indicative future claim must fall back to
     conditional or be cut. This is the gate's defense against the
     future-state failure mode above.
   - **The identity-through-disguise check** *(dormant unless the
     override supplies a recognition-state field).* For each
     proper-noun, role-noun, or relationship-term reference to
     another scene participant in dialogue or POV narration, verify
     that the speaker is on the referenced character's visible-to
     list (or not on the concealed-from list, per the override's
     semantic). If the speaker has not earned recognition, rewrite
     the reference to a description register or drop it. This is the
     gate's defense against the identity-through-disguise failure
     mode above.
   - **The verbatim-quotation check.** For each speech-attribution
     construction (*X said, "Y"; X said that Y; his exact words were;
     she put it as; to quote her*), identify the asserting speaker
     A, the attributed speaker B, and the scene in which the
     utterance was produced. Verify A's access route — presence,
     documented secondhand relay, or written source. If no access
     route is documented, rewrite to inference register, attribute to
     A's reading rather than B's words, or cut the quotation. This is
     the gate's defense against the verbatim-quotation failure mode
     above.
   - **The player-meta check** *(dormant unless the override supplies
     a mechanical-vocabulary file).* For each token in the
     override-supplied vocabulary that appears in the drafted output,
     evaluate the surrounding context against the entry's
     `flag_patterns`. If any pattern matches, rewrite per the entry's
     `rewrite_hint` or remove the mechanical reference and reframe
     in-fiction. If no pattern matches, treat as mundane use and
     allow. This is the gate's defense against the player-meta
     failure mode above.
   - **The cross-scene-conversation check.** For each anaphoric or
     shared-past construction in dialogue (*as we discussed, what we
     agreed, our conversation, like I told you, you said, remember
     when we, our agreement*), identify the implied prior
     conversation (participants, rough topic, time T' < T) and verify
     against the speaker's perception log and the scene
     log the speaker could plausibly cite. If no such conversation
     is recorded, rewrite to a new-information register, surface as
     the speaker's own prior intention, or drop the anaphoric
     clause. The same-scene exception applies **only when the invoked
     exchange is on the page** — rendered in a prior beat of this
     scene, or in its staged/saved record. Co-presence through a long
     scene grounds nothing: if you cannot **point at the beat** the
     utterance occurred in, the exception does not apply and the row
     fires. Watch for the near-miss, where the two characters did
     interact but not in the way now claimed. This is the gate's
     defense against the cross-scene-conversation failure mode above.
   - **The channel-attribution check.** For each NPC line that
     references something the PC has just done, felt, or thought,
     identify the source channel of the referenced content. If the
     source is `[bracketed]` action, the NPC perceives the action
     and its visible register — not the description's wording. If
     the source is `(parenthesized)` inner voice, the NPC perceives
     nothing from it — the thought is inaudible, unquoteable, and
     surfaces no readable tell; it is narrator-only metadata. Rewrite any
     line that quotes, paraphrases, or directly responds to
     channel-specific wording as if the PC had spoken it aloud.
     Translate the action's fictional content into the NPC's
     sensory experience of it. This is the gate's defense against
     the channel-attribution failure mode above. **Run this check
     as a literal pattern-match, not a feel-check:** extract the
     verbatim contents of every non-spoken channel in the user's
     turn and pattern-match each in-fiction character line in the
     draft against that token set. Reuse — exact or near-paraphrase
     — fires the row. A "does this feel grounded?" pass shares the
     drafter's blind spot and lets the canonical bypass through
     (*"I'm rendering the character's read of the user's manner"* —
     see the failure-mode section's *Why this row carries
     betrayal-weight*). Treat this row as the highest-betrayal-cost
     row in the suite when adjudicating gate-time priorities.
   - **The hidden-state-surfacing check (two variants).**
     *Variant A — denial.* For each negation in narration and POV
     direct thought (*there is no X, no hint of Y, no trace of Z,
     not the W one might expect, none of the [tells / marks /
     smells / sounds] of V, and it is not the [thing it would be if
     it were the hidden thing]*), identify the denied category and
     ask whether the POV would have been reading it into the
     description without the denial. If the POV has no prior reason
     to be checking for that category — if the only source of the
     category in the scene is the narrator's hidden knowledge,
     surfaced in order to be disclaimed — cut the denying clause;
     the positive description that preceded it stands. The
     legitimate exception is the negation that registers an
     *expected-but-absent* tell the POV was already braced for. The same
     check fires on the **emphatic-affirmation twin** — an intensified
     assertion of normality/safety/innocence (*perfectly ordinary,
     entirely normal, nothing to worry about, safe as houses*) by a
     narrator holding hidden contrary state; drop the intensifier and let
     plain description carry the calm.
     *Variant B — narrated withholding.* For each narrated
     non-disclosure (*does not explain how/where/why/what X, leaves
     X unsaid, without explaining X, declines to elaborate on X,
     offers no account of X, does not say what it cost / how she
     knew*), inspect the object **X**: is it stated as a fact the
     POV could not independently confirm? If yes, the line
     certifies hidden state via factive smuggling — strip it to the
     bare observable act of withholding (*she did not elaborate, and
     did not invite the question*), leaving any in-fiction dialogue
     intact and asserting nothing about the concealed content. The
     legitimate exception is non-disclosure narrated at the level of
     observable behavior only. This is the gate's defense against the
     hidden-state-surfacing failure mode above.
   - **The language-comprehension check** *(dormant unless the override
     supplies a known-languages field).* For each instance of a character
     comprehending, translating, or responding to the meaning of speech or
     writing rendered in a specific language or script, read that
     character's known-languages / literacy set. If the content's language
     or script is not in the set, rewrite so the content is unintelligible
     to them — sound or marks only, recognition of *that* it is a language
     without its meaning — route comprehension through a literate party
     member, or cut it. This is the gate's defense against the
     language-comprehension failure mode above.
   - **The disposition-surfacing check.** For each line or action that
     turns on a relationship, allegiance, or standing among others, check
     the displaying character's relationships / disposition block (including
     their read of the parties involved). If the relationship is not
     grounded there, rewrite to an observable-behavior or grounded-suspicion
     register and drop the attributed allegiance, or cut. This is the gate's
     defense against the disposition-surfacing failure mode above.
   - **The agenda-surfacing check.** For each line or action in which a
     character acts on, asserts, or forward-frames another character's goal
     or hidden plan, verify the intention is grounded in the speaker's block
     — disclosed, observably signaled, or relayed through a documented
     source. If not, recast as the speaker's own inference / projection; for
     forward *"they will do X"* framing, apply the future-state rewrite
     (conditional or inferential). This is the gate's defense against the
     agenda-surfacing failure mode above.
   - **Override-supplied patterns**, if the override defines any
     beyond the dormant-check parameters above. The override may
     extend this scan with additional forbidden tokens (e.g.,
     project-internal time indices, setting-specific terminology the
     setting must not name) and may supply parameters for the dormant
     checks above (the join-point field name for roster-at-T, the
     foresight field for future-state, the recognition-state field
     for identity-through-disguise, the vocabulary file path for
     player-meta). Treat these as additions to the same scan, not as
     a separate later pass.
3. **Post** only after the gate passes. If the gate rewrote anything,
   re-run the post-edit coherence rescan (below) on the rewrite, then
   re-run the gate, before posting.

The epistemic-pass is single-gate by design: a separate later pass could
introduce or fail to catch its own class of leak, defeating the gate.

### Director's-channel handling — scoped vs. full

The director's channel (see `{{PLUGIN_ROOT}}/engines/prose-engine/scene/references/player_input_channels.md`) modifies how
the gate evaluates the drafted response. It does not add a pass after
the gate; it changes the licensing of findings inside it.

- **Scoped directive (`{...}`).** At intake, the directive is parsed
  into a *target proposition* — the specific fact the next beat must
  establish. During drafting, the narrator marks the *realization
  span*: the sentences/clauses that exist because the directive
  demanded them, kept as small as possible. The gate then runs
  normally over the whole draft, with one modification: when a row
  fires a violation inside the realization span, the gate asks
  whether the violation is **logically entailed by the target
  proposition.** If yes, the violation is licensed and the gate
  continues. If no, the gate fails and rewrites as usual. Violations
  outside the realization span are never licensed. The entailment
  question is an honest test: if it is unclear whether a violation
  is required to land the target proposition or only convenient,
  treat it as not entailed and rewrite.
- **Irreducible conflicts (scoped).** If the target proposition
  cannot land without a second rule-break that is genuinely required
  by the first — e.g., for character B to *believe* a claim about
  fact X, B must *learn* fact X, which B did not previously know —
  the entailed second break is permitted and the consequent state
  propagates forward as established fiction. The gate may render the
  resulting beat coherently; it does not refuse the directive on
  rules grounds when the conflict is genuinely irreducible. The
  honesty discipline applies here too: an entailment that is
  *convenient* rather than *necessary* is not irreducible. Rewrite.
- **Full override (`{{...}}`).** The gate does not run against
  directive-produced content. Any beat that lands the target
  proposition under the override is permitted as drafted, including
  outcomes that violate epistemic-pass rules. Bent state propagates
  forward into subsequent beats; future beats are gated against the
  bent version, not the unbent one.
- **Choice between tiers, briefly.** Reach for `{...}` first; it is
  the right tool for the common case (a social or epistemic outcome
  that can be landed within the world's rules with minimal bending).
  Reach for `{{...}}` only when realism itself is being suspended.
  See the channel reference for examples.

### Pre-write reference check — verify callbacks before writing them in

The post-edit coherence rescan (below) catches breakage that arises
*after* a partial rewrite. A separate failure mode produces incoherence
at the drafting step itself: a callback, reference, quotation, or
analogy is reached for to fill a structural slot in a sentence-frame,
without verifying that the reference's *content* actually applies to
what the sentence is saying.

The trigger is **slot-driven reaching.** Sentence-frames have shapes
that demand particular slot-fillers: a humility-preface wants an
existing-wisdom-source to defer to; an authority-claim wants a
precedent to cite; an analogy wants a recognizable comparison; a
callback wants a recent shared moment to invoke; a parallel list or
tricolon wants a member to complete its rhythm; a "what lies ahead"
gesture wants a concrete who/what/where to name; an established motif
wants a body to recur in. When the drafting
step recognizes the shape, it can reach for any available slot-filler
by reflex — *the verse the character just quoted, the line the speaker
said an hour ago, the moment from a prior scene* — because the shape
demands a filler and there is one within reach. **The slot does not
check whether the filler actually applies. The drafter must.**

The mismatch is rarely caught by inspection of the surface line,
because the surface words around the mismatch warp to accommodate
it: clauses sprout to repair the seam, qualifiers attach to bridge the
gap, the sentence's syntax tangles around the conceptually-broken
frame. The reader sees the tangle and asks *what is this paragraph
even saying?* The drafter, after the fact, sees a sentence that
"needed a little work to land" and patches the surface — leaving the
broken frame in place.

The discipline, during the drafting step:

- **When reaching for a callback, reference, quotation, or analogy**
  to ground or shape a line, before writing it in, **verify the
  reference's content actually applies to what the line is saying.**
  Ask: *the thing this reference says — does it speak to the claim
  this sentence is making, or only to a thematically-adjacent topic
  that the sentence-frame pattern-matched against?*
- **If the reference does not fit, do not warp the sentence to fit
  it.** Either find a reference that does apply, or drop the slot
  entirely and let the line stand without the callback. A line
  without a callback is cleaner than a line with the wrong one
  forced into it.
- **The pull of a callback is the shape of the frame, not the fit of
  the content.** Trust the verification, not the pull. A reference
  that wants to be in the sentence — that the frame keeps reaching
  for — is not thereby a reference that belongs in the sentence.

The same slot-driven reaching produces **fabricated quantities** — a
concrete number pulled in to fill a slot that wants specificity (*three
days back, a dozen vials, hundreds dead*) when no source supplies the
value. Treat a reached-for quantity exactly like a reached-for callback:
verify it against grounded source data before writing it in, and if no
source supports it, render it loosely or drop it rather than inventing
precision. See the fabricated-quantity failure mode above.

The same slot-driven reaching produces **fabricated concretes** — a
who/what/where (an entity, a presence, a location, or what someone knows)
pulled into a parallel list, a "what lies ahead" gesture, or a motif echo,
and slipped into the POV's knowledge-horizon when no source grounds the
POV's knowledge of it. Treat a reached-for concrete like a reached-for
callback: before writing it in, verify the POV can ground *that entity, in
that place, as known to them.* If not, render the unknown as unknown,
relocate the concrete to where the POV actually grounds it, or drop the
slot. Legitimate forward worldbuilding — new state not claimed as POV
knowledge — is exempt. See the fabricated-concrete failure mode above.

The check is asymmetrically valuable: most lines will not trigger it
(most lines do not reach for a callback). The lines that do trigger
it are the high-resonance lines — the ones reaching for prior
material to do extra work — and those are exactly the lines a botched
reference damages most. A cost of one verification on a small subset
of lines buys protection against the kind of paragraph-level
incoherence that no surface edit can cleanly repair.

This check catches breakage that would never reach the gate, because
the unit was incoherent from its first draft. The post-edit coherence
rescan (next section) catches breakage that arises after the gate
rewrites the draft. Both are coherence disciplines; they fire at
different points in the order of operations.

### Post-edit coherence rescan — after any rewrite, before re-running the gate

Each failure-mode rewrite the gate performs — and each rewrite the
drafting step itself produces — changes only part of a sentence or
paragraph. The unedited parts may no longer cohere with the edit, or may
no longer earn their place toward the unit's purpose. Before re-running
the gate on a rewrite, scan the **whole containing unit** — the full
sentence for a word-level edit, the full paragraph for a sentence-level
edit — for two things:

1. **Coherence.** Do the unedited parts still make sense given the edit?
   Annotations, count-references (*"three words and a tail"* after the
   spoken line has been swapped), anaphoric pronouns, parallel
   structures, and trailing clauses are the most common breakages — they
   were valid against the pre-edit content and have not been updated to
   match.
2. **Purpose.** Does every sentence in the unit still earn its place
   toward what the unit is doing? A rewrite that removes a fact or
   shifts a register may strand a sentence whose only job was to set up
   the removed beat, or render a sentence tonally wrong for the new
   register.

If the rescan surfaces a break, **do not sequentially patch the new
break, then rescan, then patch the next break, ad infinitum.** That
cycle compounds error and is the most common way a clean rewrite turns
into a tangled paragraph. The trap is recognizable by its second
iteration: if a patch creates a new break that needs another patch, stop
patching.

The exit:

- **Step back to the unit's purpose.** What was this sentence or
  paragraph trying to do — convey what fact, advance what beat, hold
  what register?
- **Ask whether the unit's frame still holds, or whether the edit
  revealed that the frame was wrong.** If the rewrite cascades, the
  frame is suspect.
- **If the frame is broken, redraft the unit fresh from intent** —
  write the new version starting from purpose, not from the patched
  residue. Discard the patched version.
- **If the frame still holds, the patch is small and bounded.** One
  revision, one rescan, done.

This is a single principle: an edit is not finished until its containing
unit is coherent and on-purpose. The coherence rescan is the gate's
defense against its own rewrites silently breaking the prose around
them.

## Scope of these rules

These rules apply to **narration, scene-setting, and NPC dialogue.** They
do *not* apply to out-of-character meta-blocks the user requests
(clarifications, save confirmations, scene-mechanic explanations,
post-save bullet summaries) — those may name files and saved overlays
freely.

The qualifier **the user requests** is load-bearing. The exemption covers
blocks the player *asked for*. It does **not** cover the narrator's own
**unprompted process-narration** — *"let me check the files," "one moment
while I load X," "there's an authored thread tied to this," "there's a lot
of established material here."* Those are unrequested, player-facing
emissions and are **gated**: no infrastructure names, no hidden or
author-facing state, no designed structure the POV character cannot see.
Consulting the source material is a **silent** act — read what you need
without telling the player you are doing it; its existence and findings
are themselves author-facing only. (Full treatment: *The process-narration
slip* under the infrastructure-citation family above.)

**One exception within OOC:** the DM's answer to a player-scoped
`<…>` OOC query is gated to the player's perspective (the
knowledge-boundary and quantity rows still run, POV-anchored on the PC);
only the admin `<<…>>` tier is fully ungated. See the player-scoped OOC
carve-out in
[epistemic_discipline_checklist.md]({{PLUGIN_ROOT}}/engines/prose-engine/scene/references/epistemic_discipline_checklist.md) and
the OOC channel in [player_input_channels.md]({{PLUGIN_ROOT}}/engines/prose-engine/scene/references/player_input_channels.md).

The game override may add further in-fiction rules. As described in the
Order of Operations above, those rules are drafting constraints and
epistemic-pass extensions — never a separate verification pass after the
epistemic-pass runs.
