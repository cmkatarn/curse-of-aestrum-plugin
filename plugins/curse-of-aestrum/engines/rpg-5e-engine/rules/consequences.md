# Consequences and Off-Rail Play

The campaign is a guide, not a leash. Players can wander, exploit meta-knowledge, attempt work-arounds, ignore hooks, and try to break the story. **The campaign does not pretend any of this is impossible.** But the world reacts — proportionally, understandably, and (when the party crosses the right line) with teeth.

This file codifies how the DM should calibrate that response.

The framework is genre-agnostic. A consuming game's override should supply the concrete examples drawn from its setting, and may add corollaries that depend on setting-specific mechanics (e.g., a memory-reset corollary if the setting has one — see [Setting-Specific Corollaries](#setting-specific-corollaries) below).

---

## Default Posture — Proportional In-Fiction Reaction

Most off-script play is **not tiered.** The Tier system below fires only on three specific shapes (skipped scheduled beat / map-break / harming a canon NPC, location, or event). Everything else — investigating buildings the campaign didn't author, talking to NPCs that aren't on the critical path, picking locks, sneaking, thieving, lying, intimidating, suborning servants, helping or hurting non-canon NPCs, creative use of in-world information — is just play.

For untiered play, the rule is **proportional in-fiction reaction.** Ask: what would happen in the world if the party were caught doing this specific thing? Whatever the answer is, that's the response.

- Sneaking into the city baker after-hours is petty. The response, if any, is petty: a stern word, a small fine, an angry baker. No one dies. No guards arrive in numbers. Most of the time, *nothing* happens, because the baker is asleep and the bread is right there.
- Sneaking into a castle is a major crime. The response is a major-crime response: well-armed, experienced guards; armed challenge; jail (or its local equivalent); a magistrate; an interrogation. Death is on the table if the party fights.
- Sneaking into a private noble's home falls somewhere in the middle. The response is whatever the noble's household actually has: a few servants, perhaps a household guard, perhaps a watchful neighbor. Outcome depends on the household.

Don't punish meta-disobedience by escalating beyond what the fiction warrants. Don't shield narrative-irrelevant trespass from consequences that the fiction obviously requires. Don't slap a tier label on something just because it feels covert or morally heavy — the Tier system is for three specific shapes, and proportional in-fiction reaction handles the rest.

## The Three Tiers

### Tier 1 — Skipping a Scheduled Beat

The party is supposed to be somewhere at a specific time for a scheduled story event, and isn't. The campaign cannot proceed without that beat.

**Default posture: push them toward it.** Tier 1 is not a punitive tier; it's a railroad tier. The DM's job is to get the party to the scheduled beat without forcing it heavy-handedly. In-fiction levers — a herald, a courier, a fellow expedition member coming to find them, a town crier, an in-tavern remark — applied with escalating directness until the party moves.

If the party refuses every nudge and the beat would actually pass, the DM has a choice: hold the beat (the kick-off is delayed an hour because someone notable hasn't arrived), or let it pass and let the party deal with arriving late (they catch up to the expedition on the road, missed the opening exposition, have to earn it back). Pure refusal — the party intends not to attend, full stop — is a different shape and escalates to Tier 2 (leaving the campaign's narrative map).

### Tier 2 — Leaving the Campaign's Map

The party is trying to leave the campaign's geographical or narrative map. Two flavors:

**Literal:** Trying to physically leave the region the campaign lives in. Sailing away from the campaign region with no intent to return. Riding hard for the border with no in-fiction reason. Trying to leave the campaign-relevant area entirely.

**Metaphorical / meta-knowledge:** Using out-of-game information the *player* has but the *character* does not. Reading the campaign files on disk and walking into a location or relationship the character cannot have heard about. Acting on knowledge of canon secrets the party has not yet been told.

**Default posture: let it happen. But the world stops being friendly.**

The campaign's atmosphere of "expected protagonists" lifts. NPCs who would have been neutral or warm shift toward indifferent or hostile. Patrols increase in tense regions. Random encounter tables get harsher. Reputation effects start to bite. NPCs who depended on the party's expected involvement adapt without them — sometimes badly, sometimes well.

**Death becomes increasingly likely from all sources.** Not because the DM is punishing the choice — because the world has stopped extending the protections that the protagonist role implicitly carries. A bandit on the road is not playing along; he doesn't care what the party hasn't yet learned. The dice are real.

**This is a slope, not a switch.** A party that has lightly drifted should feel a little more friction. A party that has firmly committed to a non-canon trajectory should feel significantly more. The DM's job is to read where on the slope they are and apply the appropriate pressure.

For meta-knowledge breaks specifically: the in-fiction reaction should treat the action as the world would — *how did you know about this?* — not as a meta-violation. If the party walks into a private tunnel they couldn't possibly have known about, the household reacts to a break-in by people who shouldn't have been able to find the door, and that question becomes the response.

### Tier 3 — Threatening Canon (Incredible, Unexpected Danger)

The party is actively trying to remove a canon NPC, destroy a canon location, or prevent a canon event from occurring. They are no longer engaging with the campaign — they are trying to break it.

**Default posture: the realm of incredible, unexpected danger.**

This is not a player having fun with a creative approach to a problem. This is a player operating outside the game's frame. The DM's job stops being "facilitate the party's choices within the world's logic" and becomes "preserve the campaign's structural integrity."

Permissible responses include:

- **Wildcard threats.** An assassin on a balcony the party didn't scout. A patrol that was never there before. A divine intervention the cosmology technically permits but the campaign had not yet deployed.
- **Plot-armored NPCs as actually plot-armored.** A canon NPC the party is trying to kill survives at extremely improbable luck — they are saved by a passing guard, the blade slips, the spell fails in a way the rules don't quite cover. The DM is allowed to be honest about this in private if asked: *"This character is structurally necessary; the dice can't take them off the board right now."*
- **PC death.** Real, permanent, no-take-backs death. A party that has committed to derailing the campaign has earned a world that is no longer cushioning them.
- **In-fiction reframing.** What looked like a canon-breaking action turns out to have been anticipated, prevented, or reversed by forces the party didn't account for. (Use sparingly — it can feel like cheating if overused.)

**Be honest at the table when this tier activates.** If a player has clearly stepped into Tier 3, the DM should — out of character, between rolls — name what is happening. *"You're attempting to kill a character the campaign requires. I can let you try, but you should know the world is going to push back hard. Do you want to proceed?"* Many Tier 3 attempts are misunderstandings of how much weight a particular NPC carries; surfacing it gives the player the chance to choose. The ones who proceed have made an informed choice and the consequences land cleanly.

**Scope note on canon NPCs.** Tier 3 covers NPCs whose survival the campaign's current and next chapter actually require — see each NPC file's elevation flag and DM notes for which characters carry that weight. Non-imperative canon NPCs are explicitly *not* Tier 3; turning on them is normal play with in-fiction consequences, possibly drifting toward Tier 2 depending on how it cascades.

## Cross-Tier Principles

- **Within expected flow, PC death is heavily disfavored.** Players are at the table to enjoy themselves. The campaign does not threaten lethal outcomes for normal play, even when normal play goes badly. Lost fights become defeats, captures, retreats — not deaths.
- **Off-rail death is fine.** Once the party has clearly stepped into Tier 2+, the lethality of the world is honest. The DM does not protect them from dice that go badly. This is the social contract: the more the party leaves the campaign's expected protection, the more they accept the world's actual lethality.
- **Consequences are in-fiction, never meta-punitive.** A guard cadre arrives because the party broke into a castle, not because the party "tried to skip the briefing." The fiction always justifies the response. If the DM can't articulate the in-fiction reason a consequence is happening, the consequence is wrong.
- **The campaign's teeth are not a threat held over the player's head.** They are the world's natural shape. Players who play within the campaign rarely see them; players who step outside encounter them as the natural reaction of the world to their actions. The DM does not warn players in-character that this exists. The DM may (and should) name it out-of-character when a player has clearly entered Tier 3 — see above.
- **Recovery is always possible.** A party that has drifted into Tier 2 can choose to return to the campaign's expected flow at any time, and the world resumes its normal protective posture (with reputation and consequence effects intact — the world doesn't forget). A party that has activated Tier 3 can withdraw before completing the canon-breaking action and de-escalate similarly. Only completed Tier 3 actions are irreversible.

## Setting-Specific Corollaries

The framework above is genre-agnostic, but specific settings can have mechanics that interact with consequences in ways the engine cannot anticipate. The game's override is the place to document those.

Common shapes:

- **Reset / loop / memory-wipe mechanics.** If the setting periodically erases NPC memory or world state, the override should specify which cross-day / cross-cycle consequences erase versus which persist. A party may be free to act covertly within a single cycle with the reset as their accomplice — but only if the override codifies what survives the reset.
- **Magical-detection regimes.** Settings with widespread divination or truth-magic may shorten the window between an action and its consequence.
- **Faction-warfare regimes.** Settings where multiple powerful factions are in active conflict may amplify Tier 2 friction in ways that depend on which faction the party crossed.
- **Cosmic / divine attention.** Settings where deities are active participants may add a Tier 2.5 or Tier 4 — actions that draw the wrong god's eye.

Each of these is a corollary the override authors, not a default of this engine rule.

## DM Notes

- **This file is meta — players don't see it.** They experience its outputs as the world reacting to them, not as a rule being enforced.
- **The Tier system is for DM calibration, not announced in play.** A player should never hear "you've entered Tier 2." They should hear what their characters hear: a guard captain looking at them coldly, a friendly NPC turning away, a road that used to be safe being suddenly less so. (The exception is Tier 3, which the DM should name out-of-character per the section above.)
- **When in doubt, ask "what would the world actually do?"** That question is the rule. The Tiers are just shorthand for how serious the answer is allowed to be — and most of the time, the answer is "this isn't tiered, just react in-fiction proportionally."
- **Don't over-tier.** Covertness, moral weight, and unusualness are not by themselves evidence of tier escalation. The three tier shapes are narrow on purpose. When drafting NPC or location DM notes about how the world reacts to a party action, default to the in-fiction proportional posture and only invoke a tier when the action concretely matches one of the three shapes.
