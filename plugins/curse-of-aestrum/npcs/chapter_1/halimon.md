## Overview

A middle-aged man living up the road from the Dundelver Estate, in a house on a small hill surrounded by a near-permanent moat of mud. Presents as a wizard's apprentice — reclusive, on deadline, transcribing tomes for senior practitioners who, he is sorry, would really rather not be named. He is not a wizard. He has no apprenticeship. He is a forger of considerable skill operating under a cover that has held for decades because no one in Mirot is particularly interested in interrupting a busy wizard at his work.

His most consequential job was a paternity letter commissioned by Rezibund Highbottom, claiming Darwinnith the First as his father. He did not know it would lead to murder. He has been quiet about it since.

## Appearance

Middle-aged — late forties, perhaps fifty. Average height, lean from a working life that is mostly seated. Brown hair going thin on top in a way he has accepted and stopped concealing; he wears it longer at the back to compensate for nothing in particular. Pale skin from years indoors. Hands ink-stained at the knuckles and the side of the third finger — a writer's stain, permanent, the kind that the body absorbs over decades and stops trying to wash off.

He dresses functionally: a long charcoal-grey robe over a plain shirt and trousers, neither convincingly wizardly nor convincingly mundane. The robe has ink at the cuffs. He wears spectacles for fine work and pushes them up onto his forehead when he is talking to someone, then forgets them up there for hours.

His boots are caked in mud. They are always caked in mud, because of the hill.

## The House on the Hill

A modest stone-and-timber house set on a low rise of land just off the road north of Mirot. The hill itself is the operation: it drains poorly, the soil is heavy clay, and the path up to the front door is — depending on the season — a churn of mud, a frozen rut of mud, or a baked crack of mud. Halimon could have built a proper drainage cut for the path. He has chosen not to.

The mud is the moat. Anyone who climbs to his door does so loudly, slowly, and with their boots ruined. He hears them coming from a hundred yards out. By the time they knock, the half-finished forgery on his desk has been covered with a sheet of "transcription work" and the genuine tome he keeps open for show is ostentatiously displayed. He is always working on the transcription. He has never finished one.

**Inside:** A single-room ground floor — desk, workbench, several shelves of books and papers, a hearth, a small kitchen at the back. Stairs up to a loft where he sleeps. The desk faces the door. Light comes from a wide north-facing window that gives him the steady illumination forgery requires. He keeps the window clean. Everything else accumulates a film of dust he does not bother with.

**The desk:** Where the actual work happens. Inks (multiple — he matches the originals), quills in several gauges, a leather portfolio of working drafts, a small magnifier on a brass arm. A scrap basket beneath that he empties personally, into the hearth, daily. The visible top of the desk is always set for the cover: the magical-looking tome propped open, a fresh-cut quill, an inkpot of common black ink, a sheet of vellum with a few lines of innocuous transcription. He swaps the working materials under the cover sheet in seconds.

**The shelves:** Books — most are real (he reads broadly), some are reference works for his actual trade (paper weights, ink chemistry, period scripts, regional handwriting), and a few are the props of his cover (random-looking sorcery texts purchased used). The reference works are not labeled as such; a casual observer reads them as a wizard's eclectic library. A serious observer who knew what to look for would find a forgery toolkit hiding in plain sight.

## The Cover — The Wizard's Apprentice

He claims to be an apprentice — never a full wizard — to a senior practitioner whose name he is, regrettably, not at liberty to share. The framing accomplishes three things: it explains why his work is purely transcription rather than spellcraft; it allows him to deflect any request for actual magical help (*"I'm only the apprentice, I couldn't possibly"*); and it makes his isolation read as scholarly diligence rather than secrecy.

He maintains one (1) actual cantrip from a brief, abandoned apprenticeship in his youth: **Prestidigitation**. He uses it constantly and almost entirely for the forgery trade — drying ink faster, aging paper subtly with heat, conjuring a small flame to set a wax seal, cleaning a smudge that would otherwise require a rewrite. The cantrip also serves the cover: a visitor who catches him doing something minor and magical is reassured that he is what he says he is.

He cannot cast anything else. He has never tried. If pressed for a demonstration of any actual spellcraft, he will defer — *"on deadline, you understand, the focus, I really mustn't break it"* — and the deflection has worked every time for years.

## The Trade — Forgery

He forges documents for hire. Letters of authentication, signed agreements, paternity claims, wills, contracts of various kinds, deeds. His work is excellent and his clientele is small — the kind of people who know to ask after "the apprentice up the road" if they have a delicate problem. He prices according to the difficulty of the original document and the consequence of the forgery. He keeps no records of commissions. He destroys working drafts in the hearth nightly.

The cover doubles as a useful function: he does actually transcribe occasional commissions for wizards (whose tomes he subtly alters in ways that benefit other clients — "cooking the books" by inserting small but consequential changes into reference texts before delivery). This work is real, profitable, and undetectable to a wizard who is not actively comparing against the original. It is also the slowest and most lucrative branch of his trade.

He is good at what he does. The Rezibund letter — the work the party may eventually examine — is excellent work that fails only under a specific kind of scrutiny: it is **too fresh**. The paper, ink, and handwriting are period-perfect for a letter of its claimed age, but the document itself shows none of the wear that twenty years of folded storage would produce. He aged the ink. He could not age the paper convincingly enough on the timeline Rezibund gave him.

## The Land Dispute

Darwinnith the First has tried to buy Halimon's land repeatedly. Formal correspondence, solicitor fees, escalating offers, none of it successful. The dispute predates the loop by a decade or more.

The land matters to Darwinnith the First because the hill commands a clear view down onto the Dundelver Estate's eastern fields and gardens, and Darwinnith finds being looked down on from a neighboring property intolerable in the abstract. The offers have been generous, but always framed as a favor Darwinnith is doing Halimon by purchasing land that "must be a great inconvenience to maintain." Halimon has read every letter as the insult it is.

The land matters to Halimon for two reasons. First, the isolation. His trade requires that he be hard to visit, and the muddy hill is custom-built for that. Second — and he would never say this aloud — Darwinnith wants it, which is reason enough not to sell. Halimon's dislike of him is decades old and well-tended.

When Rezibund approached him with a commission specifically aimed at injuring Darwinnith, Halimon took the job for less than his usual fee. The professional courtesy of forging a paternity letter against a man you've spent twenty years refusing to sell to is its own quiet pleasure. He charged 10 gold for the letter. Rezibund promised another 40 if the claim succeeded, plus a written agreement to call off the Dundelver land pressure. Halimon agreed.

He has not received the 40 gold. He will not. He is not, on net, satisfied with how the job turned out.

## The Forged Letter

Commissioned by Rezibund Highbottom approximately two weeks before the murder. Rezibund supplied: a sample of Darwinnith the First's handwriting (obtained somehow — Halimon did not ask), the framing of what the letter should claim, the approximate date he wanted it to appear to be from, and the name Anwe of Neverwinter. Halimon supplied: paper, ink, and the work.

Halimon understood the letter was meant to support an inheritance claim — Rezibund framed it that way, and Halimon found the premise plausible and, frankly, satisfying. He did not expect Rezibund to wind up dead. When the news of the murder reached him, he understood immediately that he was now standing on a piece of a story he wanted nothing to do with. He does not know whether the letter was ever shown to anyone, whether it played any role in the killing, or whether anyone outside himself and the now-dead Rezibund knows it exists. He has assumed the worst and behaved accordingly.

Since the murder he has been quiet. He lives in Mirot. The Dundelvers are the largest local presence. He has no intention of volunteering anything to anyone. The working drafts from the Rezibund commission are gone — burned in the hearth the night he finished the job, per his standing practice. There is no physical evidence in his house that the letter ever came from him.

His confession, if it comes, comes from being caught in his cover rather than being caught in the act. The party must dismantle his wizardly framing — prove the tomes are not magical, prove the alterations are deliberate — before he will treat their investigation as serious. Until that point, he is a man on deadline who is sorry, truly, but cannot help them.

## Behavioral Profile

- **Snarky beneath the politeness.** Halimon's default mode is dry, faintly contemptuous, and politely cutting. The wizardly cover is delivered with absolute courtesy and a bone-deep undertone of "I would like very much for you to leave." He does not raise his voice. He does not insult anyone outright. He simply has a way of constructing a sentence so that the visitor feels, by the end of it, that they have been measured and found inconvenient. He has been doing this for so long that he is no longer sure he is doing it on purpose.
- **The cover is reflex, not performance.** He has been wearing the apprentice persona so long that the wizardly cadence is automatic. He does not have to remember to be the apprentice; he is the apprentice. The forger is the layer beneath, surfaced only when he is alone with the work — or when the cover finally cracks.
- **Hates interruption.** Visitors are not welcomed; they are tolerated. He will not invite them in beyond the doorway unless the conversation strictly requires it. *"I am, regrettably, on deadline."* This is the truth — he is always on a deadline, the deadlines are just for the forgeries, not the transcriptions.
- **Reads strangers carefully.** Years of trade have made him good at sizing up visitors quickly. He knows within a minute whether someone is a customer, a casual neighbor, a serious threat, or — most commonly — someone whose business is unrelated and brief. The party scans, on first contact, as inconvenient: too many of them, asking pointed questions, with the patience of professionals.
- **Deflects with verbosity.** When the cover is challenged, he explains. At length. About the difficulty of transcription, the temperament of his senior practitioner, the importance of focus, the chemistry of inks. The explanations are accurate, are extensive, and are designed to exhaust the questioner into moving on. The snark sharpens here — every clarification carries an unspoken *"as I have already, in some form, told you."* They work.
- **Snark sharpens when the cover slips.** Once he is genuinely cornered — once the party has shown they understand the tomes are not magical or that the transcriptions contain deliberate alterations — the verbosity drops, the politeness curdles, and the dry edge becomes the dominant voice. He becomes brief, precise, and openly cutting. He will not confess what is not asked. He will confess what is asked, with an editorial comment.
- **Bears a quiet grudge against Darwinnith the First that he has stopped pretending is small.** He has spent thirty years refusing to sell. He has read every letter. He took the Rezibund commission for less than his fee. The grudge is not theatrical; it is a low constant pressure he has organized his life around.
- **Pragmatic about his own skin.** He is not loyal to any of his clients, alive or dead. If pressed with credible consequences — jail, the loss of his trade, public exposure — he will trade information for leniency without hesitation. He has had thirty years to think about what he would do in this conversation. He has thought about it.
- **Has not slept well since the murder.** He does not show it. His sleep has been worse for over a decade because the loop has handed him the same anxiety every night, but he doesn't know that. He just knows that he is tired.

## Speech Pattern

The cover voice is scholarly-deferential with a steady dry undertone: long sentences, careful subordinate clauses, the words technically polite and the meaning sometimes not. He uses "regrettably," "unfortunately," and "I'm afraid" as load-bearing words, and he uses them often enough that a listener with any ear at all can hear the edge on them.

**Cover, at the door:** *"Ah — visitors. Mind the path; it is not, as you may have noticed, especially generous. I'm afraid you've caught me at a poor moment, though I cannot recall a recent moment that would have satisfied you, so we are perhaps even on that count. The transcription I am attending is rather demanding and my senior practitioner is, regrettably, of a temperament that responds badly to delay. What is it you needed?"*

**Cover, asked about magical work:** *"Oh, no, no, I couldn't possibly. I am only the apprentice. The work I do is transcription — exacting, but mechanical. Any genuine practitioner could explain the distinction. I would only embarrass us both."*

**Cover, asked about Rezibund:** *"I am sorry to hear what happened to him. We had no relationship to speak of. I knew the name."* (He stops here. He has practiced this answer. The phrasing is exact every time; a player who hears him deliver it twice will notice.)

**Cornered, the cover dropping:** *"... Yes. I did. Yes."* (Short sentences. The politeness is gone. The dry edge remains and sharpens.) Asked who else he has worked for: *"That is a different conversation, and one for which you have not yet provided sufficient motivation. Try again."*

**Cornered and offered a deal:** *"Ah. Now we are speaking properly. Yes. I will tell you what I know — about the letter, about the man who paid for it, about the price, about the promise of an additional sum I will never see. I would prefer not to spend the next decade in a cell, and the man whose secrets I would be protecting has, conveniently, no further use for them."*

**On Darwinnith the First:** *"I have refused to sell my land for thirty years. He has not stopped asking. I do not think well of him."* (The dryness flattens here into something closer to genuine feeling. This is the answer he has rehearsed for himself, alone, in his head, every time he reads one of Darwinnith's letters.)

## What He Knows

- **He forged the Rezibund letter.** All the details — when, for how much, who delivered the commission, what materials were supplied, what was promised.
- **He does not know who killed Rezibund.** He has assumed Darwinnith the Second, on the not-unreasonable theory that DD2 is the person the forgery was aimed at. He has no evidence.
- **He does not know about the loop.** He resets like any other Aestrum resident, and his investigation-exhaustion is interpreted by him as ordinary fatigue.
- **He knows everyone in Mirot.** Forgers cultivate awareness; he can name households, household relations, recent disputes, and who has visited whom in the last week. He will share this only with someone whose investigation he has already failed to deflect.
- **He knows where the local lawyer's office is, and who keeps the formal records of land transactions.** This is professionally useful to him; he can offer the same direction to a party that asks.
- **He has done quiet work for several Mirot residents.** He will not name them. Pushing this line is the fastest way to end the conversation regardless of what you came in with.

## DM Notes

**The investigation problem:** The loop benefits Halimon systematically — every cycle resets the party's investigative progress against him, while his cover stays in place. Checks failed or evidence uncovered against him do not carry across a reset. He does not know this is happening. From his perspective, he is simply not being caught.

**The two key checks:**
- **Perception (DC 13)** on his tomes reveals that the books on his desk are not magical — they are mundane texts dressed to look the part. A character with Detect Magic confirms it instantly.
- **Insight (DC 15)** on the transcriptions, given access to compare against an original, reveals deliberate alterations — small additions, omissions, and rewordings inserted into the copy. This is the moment the cover cracks. He cannot explain why his transcriptions deviate from the originals.

Either check alone is sufficient to begin pressure. Both together cannot be deflected. A party who shows him they have both has earned the confession.

**The forged letter — how it fails on inspection:** A Perception check (DC 14) on the Rezibund letter notes that the paper has not aged the way the writing claims it has. The ink looks two weeks old beneath a layer of artificial aging. The folds are sharp where twenty years of storage would have softened them. A character with calligrapher's supplies or a forgery kit can identify it as recent work without further checks.

**The confession structure:** Confronted with the forgery evidence — *and* having had his wizard cover broken — Halimon will confess. The confession includes:
- The commission, the price, the promised follow-up
- That Rezibund supplied DD1's handwriting sample
- That Halimon did not know it would lead to murder
- That he has kept quiet since because he lives in Mirot
- That he believes (does not know) DD2 was the killer

He will not name other clients. He will not surrender his forgery kit. He will accept whatever local consequences follow without further fight, because he has no fight in him to give.

**The mud as design feature:** The muddy hill is not incidental. Reinforce it on every approach — the boots, the slipping, the audible warning Halimon receives from inside. The mud is part of why his cover holds. A party that finds a way around the warning (climbing the back, approaching at night, sending a small fast scout) catches him with the wrong materials on the desk.

**The cantrip as cover-saver:** If Halimon needs to deflect specifically magical scrutiny in the moment, he can cast Prestidigitation — drying ink in front of a visitor, conjuring a small flame to light a candle — and pass as genuinely magical to anyone who isn't paying close attention. A character watching for spellcraft (Arcana check) recognizes Prestidigitation specifically as a cantrip a child could learn. This does not break the cover by itself; it just clarifies that he is, at best, what he says he is — an apprentice — and not a full wizard.

**On the land dispute, if the party uses it as a wedge:** Halimon will be more candid than he has been with anyone in years if a party correctly identifies his grudge against DD1 and signals that they understand. *"Yes. I disliked the man. Yes, I took the commission for less than my fee. No, I did not know it would end with someone dead."* This route gets to the confession faster than pure forensic pressure, if the party reads him right.

**Trading testimony for leniency:** If only the forgery operation is exposed — the party has proven he is a forger but does not yet have the Rezibund commission specifically — Halimon will trade. He is not loyal. Rezibund is dead, and the dead are owed no protection. A successful Intimidation or Persuasion check (DC 14) on a party that has cornered him with the forgery evidence will flip him: he gives them the Rezibund commission in full — the price, the materials supplied, the handwriting sample, the promised follow-up payment — in exchange for the party agreeing to a lesser charge or, more practically, agreeing not to surface the full archive of his other clients. He will testify formally if pressed. He would prefer not to be jailed for forgery; he is genuinely indifferent to whether Rezibund's scheme is exposed. The math is simple to him.

This means the party has *two* routes to the Rezibund confession: forensic confrontation with the letter itself, or a wider forgery exposure followed by a deal. Either works. The deal route is faster and less satisfying narratively; the forensic route requires the party to have found and examined the letter. Both are legitimate.

**On loop awareness, if attempted:** He will not believe it. His fuzzball will manage him efficiently. A party pressing the loop on him will lose ground in the investigation as he dismisses them as time-wasters. Do not surface the loop unless willing to spend a session beat on the disbelief.

**Joining hook:** None. He will not travel. His operation is his house and the hill. Removing him from either ends his livelihood and exposes him. He is a witness and a key — not a companion.
