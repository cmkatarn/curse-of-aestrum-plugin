## Overview

A farmer on a small plot half a mile outside Rockwood proper, off the road that wanders south toward the highlands. Mid-forties, weathered to look older. His wife died not long before the loop began; her grave is the freshest of the four already in the row at the back of his field. The two oldest graves, well-maintained but grown over with low grass, belong to his father and mother. The fourth grave is his own — fully dug, sized to him, the spoil mound covered with a tarp weighted with stones. He spends most of the daylight hours working on a fifth, smaller grave at the end of the row. That one is for his son, Cal, who is six years old and at this hour is somewhere down by the creek that runs the western edge of the property, throwing rocks at frogs.

He is not planning to kill the boy. He is digging the grave so the boy will not have to dig it himself someday — the way Bram did for his own father, and then his mother, and then his wife, and now (he has decided) for himself in advance, because somebody has to and he won't put it on Cal. The son's grave is the logical extension of that thinking. Bram has worked it out carefully and arrived at a conclusion that is, by any outside measure, deeply wrong, and he holds it with the calm conviction of a man who has had a long time to think it through alone.

He is not dangerous. He loves his son. He is a depressed man doing what he believes is the last kindness he can prepare in advance.

## Appearance

Tall and rangy, the kind of farmer who has not had a full meal in weeks and the weight is showing in the hollows of his cheeks and the way his trousers sit. Dark hair, going early to grey at the temples, cropped short under a flat wool cap he never takes off. A week of stubble going on two. Pale blue eyes set deep, with a permanent redness at the rims — not from crying in the moment, from a long quiet stretch of crying in the evenings. Hands are wrecked: blistered, calloused, ground-in dirt at every knuckle, two fingernails on the right hand missing and not yet grown back.

He wears the same clothes every day — heavy brown trousers patched at both knees, a once-white linen shirt now the color of weak tea, a wool waistcoat unbuttoned and stained dark at the front from leaning on the shovel handle. Boots cracked across the toe. No belt; a length of hemp rope holds the trousers up. Around his neck on a leather cord, a small wooden carving of a horse, badly whittled — Cal made it for him last winter.

His shovel is good. He keeps the shovel good. Edge filed, handle oiled, head clean. The shovel is the only thing he has put effort into maintaining in months.

## The Plot

A modest holding — maybe six acres of mostly-fallow field, a barn that needs the roof seen to, a small farmhouse with a sagging porch, a vegetable garden gone to weed at the side of the house. A creek along the west edge. Sheep enclosure at the back, currently containing two sheep where there used to be a dozen; the rest were sold or died or wandered off after his wife passed and he stopped keeping the fence patched.

The graves are at the back of the field, in a row running north-south along a low rise that gets the morning sun. Reading from the older end:

- **First grave:** *My Paw.* Grown over with grass, edges still defined. A flat stone at the head, no carving — Bram cannot read or write, and never paid anyone to carve. He maintains it. The grass is trimmed; the stone is set straight.
- **Second grave:** *My Maw.* Same condition. Same flat stone. Same maintained edge.
- **Third grave:** Wife. Mound still raised, earth still dark, the grass not yet retaken it. A flat stone, freshly set. He does not refer to her by name when he talks about her — he calls her *my woman,* or *her,* or sometimes nothing at all, the sentence trailing off and being finished by the shovel going back into the dirt.
- **Fourth grave:** *His own.* Dug to full depth, sized to him, sides clean. A tarp pegged over the opening to keep the rain out. The spoil mound beside it is covered and weighted. He climbs down into it sometimes to check the dimensions. He has lain in it once, to be sure. He found it fit fine.
- **Fifth grave (in progress):** *His son's.* Shallow yet — maybe two feet down, child-sized footprint marked out at the bottom with twine and pegs. He works on it most of the daylight hours, slow and methodical, the way a man works on something he has no rush to finish but no intention of abandoning.

He has not made a stone for the fifth grave yet. He has thought about what to put on it, in his own head, and settled on *My Baw* — the same simple phrasing as the others. He has not said this out loud to anyone.

## Behavioral Profile

- **Depressed in the bone-deep, settled way.** Not weepy, not raging, not actively suicidal in any urgent sense — just hollowed out. The grief has finished its sharp phase and become the climate. He does not perform sorrow; he simply is in it. Speaks slowly when not animated, moves slowly, eats when he remembers to eat, sleeps badly.
- **Calmly certain about the graves.** When asked what he is doing, he will tell whoever asks, plainly and without embarrassment. He has thought it through. He believes his reasoning is sound and is happy to walk a listener through it: *somebody digs every grave; he doesn't want Cal to dig his; he doesn't want Cal to dig Cal's either, because Cal would have to do that alone someday and that's a thing he can spare the boy by doing it now.* He does not understand that this lands on listeners as horrifying. To him it is foresight.
- **Loves his son openly.** Will light up briefly when Cal is mentioned, then dim again. Talks about him with simple specifics — what Cal said this morning, what Cal is good at, what Cal made him last winter (the horse around his neck). The love is the most intact part of him. It is also, in a way the party may need to gently raise with him, the reason the fifth grave is wrong.
- **Has had a long time to think and not many people to talk to.** Will engage in conversation readily and at length once someone slows down to listen. Rockwood neighbors have stopped coming around — they did at first, after the wife, but the graves got worse and they got uncomfortable and they stopped. He notices and does not blame them. He will not chase a conversation; he will receive one fully.
- **Not loop-aware.** Resets like everyone else. Each morning he wakes in the farmhouse, hears Cal moving in the next room, gets up, eats whatever is to hand, picks up the shovel, walks out to the row, and starts again on the fifth grave. The progress he made yesterday is not there. He does not notice. The shovel feels the same in his hand either way.
- **Receptive to gentle pushback, resistant to brisk pushback.** A party member who simply tells him he is wrong will get a slow nod and a return to digging — he has heard variations and is not moved by being told. A party member who sits down beside him and talks with him about Cal specifically — what Cal would want, what Cal is going to need — can move him. He will not stop digging in one conversation. He will think about it. (Across cycles, the conversation does not persist.)
- **Will not harm his son and will not harm himself today.** The fifth grave is, in his head, a project on a horizon — *someday,* not today. He intends to finish digging it and then keep it covered like his own, ready. He is not in acute crisis. He is in chronic, settled, wrong-conclusion sorrow.
- **Practical fearlessness around death.** He has buried four people. The shovel is a familiar tool. He talks about graves the way another farmer talks about a fence line. This is not bravado; it is calluses.

## Speech Pattern

Speech tumbles out in a single rolling cadence — words mashed together at high speed, consonants swallowed, the 'g' dropped from every '-ing', the 'h' dropped from the front of words. Vowels are broadened and rounded: *boy* becomes *baw,* *father* becomes *faw* or *faddur,* *mother* becomes *maw,* *house* becomes *ouse,* *thing* becomes *ting.* 'Th' softens to 't' or 'd' through most of his vocabulary. Sentences run on without breath, several clauses stitched together by *an'* and *roigh'* and *innit,* then land hard on a single final word that he stops on for emphasis. Heavy use of *fookin'* as a connective particle — closer to punctuation than profanity, scattered through every other sentence without thought. Repetition is a tool: he says the important word twice, the second time slower. *Proper deep, deep proper.*

Largely unintelligible to outsiders on first hearing. The meaning emerges when a listener stops trying to parse individual words and lets the cadence carry — the rhythm tells you which beat is the point, and the point is usually the last word he lands on. He is not aware that he is hard to understand; it is simply how everyone he grew up around spoke. If a listener asks him to repeat himself he will, slower, with no irritation — and it will be almost as unintelligible the second time, but a few more nouns will come through.

He does not curse with malice. The *fookin's* are warm even when the sentence is sad.

**On the work, casually, when asked what he's doing:**
*"Diggin', diggin', what's it look like, fookin' diggin' innit. Got me paw down that end, me maw next 'im, me woman there roigh', meself there 'cos that's where I'm goin' some day, an' this one 'ere, this little one, this'll be for me baw. Me baw Cal, the one down the crick. Good lad. Proper good. Proper."*

**On why he is digging his son's grave:**
*"'Cos somebody fookin' 'as to, ya see? Somebody's got to dig 'em. I done me paw, I done me maw, I done me woman, I'm gonna do meself — that's what that one's for, that's me own there roigh' — an' that leaves Cal. An' Cal's not gonna dig 'is own, is 'e? That's a fookin' awful job for a lad. Awful. So I'm doin' it now. Now while I can. While the back's still 'oldin' up. Save 'im the doin' of it. That's all it is. That's all."*

**On the wife (asked, gently):**
He stops digging. Wipes his nose on his sleeve. Looks at the third stone for a long moment.
*"She were a good woman. Good. Best of us. Went quick at the end, mercy that, the only fookin' mercy in it. I put 'er in there meself. Wouldn't let no one else. ... You don't want to be 'earin' about 'er, you don't. Let 'er rest."*
Returns to the shovel.

**On Cal, when asked about him:**
The first warmth in his face since the conversation began.
*"Ah, me baw, me baw — Cal, that's 'im, six year old, sharp as a fookin' tack 'e is. Made me this 'ere — see? 'Orse. Said it were an 'orse. I said roigh' that's a fookin' 'orse innit, fine 'orse, an' 'e were that proud. 'E's down the crick now most like, throwin' rocks at the fookin' frogs. Frogs deserve it I reckon. Cheeky bastards."*
A pause. The warmth drains.
*"That's why this one's gotta be done. 'Im. So 'e don't 'ave to."*

**Gently challenged (someone sits with him and asks if Cal would want this):**
He does not answer immediately. He leans on the shovel. The cadence slows; the slur thickens; the *fookin's* drop out.
*"...I dunno. I dunno that. Maybe 'e wouldn't. Maybe 'e'd not want it. ... I done it for me paw, an' me paw never asked me to. I just done it 'cos it needed doin'. Same as this. Same. ... But I dunno what 'e'd want. I never asked 'im. I dunno that I could ask 'im."*
Long silence. He goes back to digging, but slower.

**Briskly told he is wrong:**
A small nod. No argument. The shovel goes back in.
*"Roigh'. Roigh' you are."*
He resumes work. He has heard it before.

## What He Knows

- **The farm and his family.** His own history, his parents' deaths (illness, years ago), his wife's death (illness, more recently — he is vague on specifics and the party may not need to press), Cal's age, habits, the boy's whereabouts on the property.
- **Rockwood neighbors, distantly.** He used to know them better. He no longer does. He can name a few — the family that used to bring bread by, the man who buys his last good ram each spring — but he is not a current source on Rockwood gossip. The neighbors have stopped engaging with him.
- **Nothing about the murder investigation.** He has no connection to Rezibund, the Dundelvers, Mirot, or anything north of the four-way crossroads. He lives at a remove from town affairs and has done so since well before his wife died.
- **Nothing about the loop.** Resets like everyone else.

He is just past the point where losing matters more than the standing between.

## DM Notes

- **What he is and is not.** Bram is an atmosphere character and a moral-weight encounter, not an investigation-relevant NPC. He does not hold information the party needs. He is a person they meet, and what they do with him — whether they engage, whether they push back, whether they sit with him — is a window into them, not a quest gate. Treat any in-conversation pushback they manage as the win condition; the grave does not need to be filled in or refused for the encounter to mean something.
- **The fifth grave is not an active threat.** Bram is not going to harm Cal. Played wrong, this character risks reading as a horror beat or a clue that the boy is in danger today. He is not. The horror is that a depressed loving father has reasoned himself into something genuinely awful as an act of foresight, and the surrounding community has quietly let him drift there. If the party reads it as imminent danger and tries to intervene urgently, Bram is confused by the urgency — *"No, no, not today, not for years, what're ya on about?"* — and the misread can be resolved by listening to him.
- **Cal.** The boy is present on the land — by the creek, in the barn, near the sheep enclosure, wherever the DM places him at the hour the party arrives. He is six, cheerful, unremarkable, has no awareness that his father is digging him a grave. Bram has not told him and the neighbors have not told him. Cal knows his mother is buried at the back of the field; he visits the stone sometimes. He has not asked questions about the other holes yet. The party meeting Cal first, before they meet Bram, is a viable framing — the boy can lead them to "me da, 'e's out back diggin'" without any sense that anything is wrong.
- **The dialect.** Lean into the dialect as written. It is hard to read and harder to speak aloud — that is the point. The party should be working a little to understand him, the way the man is working a little to be understood. If a player asks for a repeat, give them a repeat; let a few extra nouns come through; do not translate. The grief lives inside the rhythm, not the dictionary.
- **Reset behavior.** Each cycle, Bram restarts the fifth grave from the same shallow start. A party that has had the gentle-pushback conversation with him on a previous cycle returns to find him digging again, with no memory of them. This is intended to land hard — it is the loop's cruelty made small and domestic. The party may feel they fixed something and they did not; the fix did not persist; Bram is back at the shovel. The encounter is reusable; the *resolution* is not.
- **On reset:** Standard. No cross-cycle memory. Same shovel, same shallow grave, same boy by the creek. The wife stays buried. His own grave stays dug. Cal stays six. Today stays today.
- **Joining hook:** None. He will not leave the plot. The graves are here, Cal is here, the shovel is here. He is fixed terrain.
