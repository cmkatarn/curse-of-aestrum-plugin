## Overview

Apothecary of Mirot. Sixty-something, plump, gray-streaked hair, the kind of practical good humor that comes from decades of selling the same hangover cure to the same retired magistrate. She has been the village's herbalist, alchemist, and unofficial doctor for over twenty years. She knows her neighbors' ailments, their families, what they buy and when, and which of them she is supposed to politely not ask follow-up questions of. Mirot is a community of retirees with money and minor complaints; she has built a comfortable life serving exactly that.

She is also a more capable alchemist than her quiet practice would suggest. She trained in Neverwinter, was a journeyman in the alchemical guild there for a decade, and left for reasons she will not volunteer. The skill came with her. The volume of her work did not.

Two threads tie her to the campaign: she sold the rat poison that killed Rezibund Highbottom (in good faith — verbal order, stated purpose, a poison she sells regularly to the same households), and she saw Rezibund with another man on the road to the Dundelver Estate the evening of the murder. She will be helpful about both, once asked.

## Appearance

Mid-to-late fifties. Short, plump, comfortable in her own body in a way that suggests she stopped fighting it in her forties and is glad she did. Calishite heritage — deep golden-brown skin, dark eyes, the features and bone structure of southern Calimshan — though she was born in the north and the heritage shows in appearance more than in voice or manner. Hair pinned up in a loose practical bun, dark brown going to grey, with a few escaped strands she does not bother to tame. Brown eyes, lively, set in a face that smiles easily and lines deeply when she does.

She dresses for the work: a long apron over a plain dress, sleeves rolled to the elbow, the apron pockets full of small tools and pouches. Her hands are stained at the cuticles — green, brown, occasionally rust-red — from the day's work. She has the broad-knuckled hands of someone who has been chopping, grinding, and stirring for forty years. She wears no rings. The kettle is always on.

## The House and Shop

She lives and works in the same building — a modest two-storey house on the main street of Mirot, the apothecary occupying the ground floor and her living quarters above. The front door is painted a faded green. The sign beside it reads simply *Landon's Apothecary*, in lettering she had repainted ten years ago and has not touched since. Customers walk into her home; she does not maintain the separation, and after twenty years no one in Mirot expects her to.

**The front room (shop):** Where customers come in. A long counter, a wall of small drawers labeled in her own hand, shelves of glass and earthenware jars, bundled herbs hanging from beams overhead. The smell is layered — mint and chamomile most prominently, then lavender, then the sharper notes of medicinal preparations and the faint mineral tang of alchemical reagents. A small iron stove against the far wall keeps the kettle hot. Two chairs near the stove for customers who want to sit while she fetches their order.

**The back workroom:** A larger space than the front would suggest, opening off the rear of the shop. A long workbench under a north-facing window for the same reason Halimon has one — steady light. Mortars and pestles in three sizes, a small distillation apparatus that gets less use than it could, drying racks, a careful filing system of recipes and notes. This is where she does the work that earned her the journeyman's papers in Neverwinter. She does not show this room to customers unless she has a reason to.

**Upstairs:** Her living quarters. Small sitting room, bedroom, a second smaller room that was once her late husband's study and is now where she keeps her personal library of mystery novels and crime broadsheets. The sitting-room window faces north — looking out, across the rear lane, toward the Dundelver Estate. She sits there most evenings with tea and a book.

**The garden:** Behind the house. A walled herb garden, maybe forty feet square, organized by use rather than appearance. She tends it herself, every morning. It is where she is, more often than not, when a customer comes looking for her.

## Practice

The Mirot apothecary trade is most of what she does. Sleep aids, joint salves, digestive teas, hangover cures, mild pain remedies. Tonics. The retired estate-holders are her steady clientele and they have the same complaints as anyone their age. She knows what works on whom.

The other half of the trade is the practical work the village needs and rarely thinks about. Rat poison. Wasp deterrents. Treatments for sick livestock. Tinctures for cuts that have started to look bad. Burn salves for the bakery accidents. She rarely refuses a request that comes with a clear purpose. She doesn't pry; an apothecary who pries does not stay in business.

The deeper work — actual alchemy, the kind that produces magical effects — she does on commission only. She has the training. She rarely has the materials, and she has not had a client willing to pay journeyman rates in Mirot in fifteen years. When a client does arrive with rare reagents and a real request, she lights up in a way her regulars do not get to see.

## What She Saw (Rezibund's Last Evening)

Her sitting-room window upstairs looks across the rear lane at the Dundelver Estate — close enough that the lit windows of the estate's ground floor are clearly visible after dark. She knows the Dundelvers well. She knows what each window opens onto. The estate's dining room is on the side facing her house.

The evening Rezibund was killed, Rose was at her window with tea and a book, as she often is. The Dundelver dining room was lit. She glanced over more than once across the course of the evening — the way one does, when there is movement in a familiar window. She saw two figures at the table. One was Rezibund, sitting on the near side of the room, in profile from her vantage. The other was a man sitting opposite him — angled almost entirely away from her window, his back and one shoulder visible, his face never turned in a direction she could read. A broad man. Dark-haired, she thought, though the candlelight and the distance made the colors uncertain. He gestured occasionally. He did not turn around.

She knows the Dundelvers. She would recognize Darwinnith the Second on sight. She did not recognize this man — but she did not see his face, and she did not see his front. He could have been Darwinnith. He could have been any number of men of that general build. She has thought about it carefully and concluded, honestly, that she cannot say.

She did not think the dinner was unusual at the time. The Dundelvers entertain. She finished her tea and went to bed. She did not learn of Rezibund's death until the following morning, when the village began to talk.

What she can describe: the time (well after sunset, the window lit), the figures (two), Rezibund's posture (relaxed, animated, talking with hands), the second man (broad build, dark hair likely, back to her throughout). She cannot place him as Darwinnith and she cannot rule him out. Combined with Tovy Holm's eyewitness account in Rockwood, the picture firms up; on its own, her testimony establishes only that Rezibund had a visitor that evening.

## What She Sold (The Poison)

The rat poison that killed Rezibund came from her shop. She sold the same compound to both the Dundelver Estate and the Tahen Estate within the last several weeks, verbally, for a stated shared rat problem. No purchase slip. This is normal for her — verbal orders are routine for regular customers, and the rat problem in Mirot is a known shared concern across multiple households this season.

She will disclose the sales freely on interrogation. She has nothing to hide. The shared order is, structurally, the issue: it dilutes the poison as evidence against any one suspect, because both estates had legitimate access. She is aware enough of this implication to say so without prompting — *"if you're asking me whose poison did it, I cannot tell you. Both households had the same compound from the same batch. I would not stake an accusation on it."*

She is also genuinely shaken once she understands her work was used to kill someone. She processes it without losing composure, but the conversation visibly changes her. She will help the party more freely from that point — not because she feels guilty (she did nothing wrong), but because someone she sold to weaponized her trade, and she would like to know who.

## Behavioral Profile

- **Delighted by a good murder.** Rose loves a mystery. She has a shelf of crime broadsheets and detective novels upstairs and reads them as fast as she can acquire them. An actual murder in the village is, on net, the most exciting thing that has happened to her in years. She will not perform sorrow she does not feel — she did not know Rezibund well enough to mourn him — and she will not pretend that being questioned is anything other than thrilling. She will ask the party, hopefully and with a grin, whether she is a suspect. She will be a little disappointed if the answer is no.
- **Warm and forthcoming.** No cover, no agenda. She is exactly who she presents as: a competent village apothecary who knows her trade and her neighbors. The party gets honest answers in plain language — answers that are, when the topic turns to the murder, delivered with visible relish.
- **Practical about her business.** She sells what is asked for as long as the purpose is plausible. She does not interrogate her customers about their stated uses, because no apothecary who does that stays in business. She will defend this professional norm if pressed — *"if I refused every order I could imagine misused, I would refuse nearly everything I sell."*
- **Observant of her neighbors.** Forty years of apothecary work has taught her to notice who is buying what and when. She does not gossip in the abstract — discretion is part of professional norm — but a legitimate murder investigation, in her view, suspends ordinary discretion entirely. She will share specifics, theories, suspicions, and patterns with the investigating party freely. This is what mysteries are for.
- **Quietly proud of her training.** She rarely brings up Neverwinter, but she will if the conversation gets there. She is not bitter about her quieter Mirot practice; she chose it. She is, however, delighted when a commission demands the deeper work, and she does not hide the delight.
- **Reliably honest about facts; loose with theories.** She will not lie about what she sold, what she saw, or what she knows. She will, however, gleefully speculate when invited — and even when not invited — about who might have done it and why. *"Have you considered the wife? It is always the wife, statistically. Although the wife is in Rockwood, so perhaps not. The brother, then. There is always a brother."*
- **Shaken by complicity, briefly.** When she understands her poison killed someone she goes quiet for a moment — the murder-fan delight pauses, the professional registers what has happened, the quiet is a real quiet — and then she comes back. *"Well. That is — yes. I see."* She does not unravel. The realization actually deepens her interest, not lessens it: now she is a part of the puzzle, which is more or less the dream.

## Speech Pattern

Plain, warm, herbalist-precise. She talks the way someone talks who has explained the same dosages to elderly customers for forty years — clear, unhurried, with a habit of mild repetition for emphasis. She uses "dear" and "love" as ambient warmth on regulars; she drops it for serious conversation. Her register is older than she is; she picked it up from her own mentor and has never seen a reason to change.

When the conversation turns to the murder, the register shifts. She leans forward. The cadence picks up. She becomes a woman in the middle of a story she has been waiting to hear told.

**On her trade, casually:** *"What you want for that, dear, is the willow bark. Twice a day, with food, not on an empty stomach or it'll turn on you. Two coppers."*

**First questioned about the murder:** *"Oh, oh — you're investigating. Of course you are. Sit down, both of you, sit down. Tea? Yes. Now. Am I a suspect? I would dearly love to be a suspect. Tell me everything you have so far and I will tell you what I know."*

**On the poison, when asked:** *"Yes, the Dundelvers have a standing order. So do the Tahens. The rats this season have been bad, and the compound I sell is the one that works on what we have. I sold to both households within the last three weeks. I do not keep slips for regulars, but I can tell you the amounts if you need them. Make of that what you will. I have my own theories, of course, but I expect you would like to do your own work first."*

**On what she saw:** *"I was at my window. Upstairs. The window looks toward the Dundelver dining room — you can see it plainly from there, after dark, when the candles are lit. There were two figures at the table. One was Rezibund; I saw his profile clearly. The other was a man sitting opposite him, broad-shouldered, dark-haired I think, with his back to me the whole time. He did not turn around once that I noticed. I would know Darwinnith the Second on sight; I do not know if it was him. I did not see his face."*

**Asked if it could have been Darwinnith the Second:** *"It could have been. I will not tell you it was, and I will not tell you it wasn't. The build was a match. So would the build of a dozen men I could name. Bring me more, and I will tell you whether it firms up."*

**Speculating, when invited (or not):** *"Have you considered the wife? It is always the wife, statistically — although in this case the wife is in Rockwood, which complicates matters. The brother, then. There is always a brother. Or — oh, the inheritance. Was there an inheritance? There is always an inheritance."*

**When she realizes her poison killed Rezibund:** A pause. She sets down whatever she was holding. The murder-fan brightness goes out of her face for a moment and she looks every one of her years. *"Ah. Well. That is — yes. I see."* Then, after a breath, the brightness returns at lower intensity, more thoughtful: *"Well. I suppose that makes me part of it, doesn't it. Tell me what you need from me."*

## What She Knows

- **The poison sale, in detail.** Compound, quantities, dates, recipients. Both estates. Verbal orders. The shared rat problem.
- **What she saw the evening of the murder.** Two figures in the lit Dundelver dining room, seen from her upstairs window. Rezibund in profile; an unidentified broad-shouldered man with his back to her throughout. She knows the time within a window of about two hours (the dinner ran long enough that she glanced over multiple times).
- **Rezibund as an occasional customer.** Not a regular. He came in once or twice for tinctures for small wounds — a cut on the hand, a graze from a fall. Polite, brief, paid in coppers. She knew him as a Mirot man more than as a customer.
- **The Dundelvers as long-standing customers.** She has sold to the household for over a decade. She would recognize Darwinnith the First, Darwinnith the Second, and household staff on sight. The Dundelvers' habits are familiar to her — when they entertain, when they travel, who their visitors usually are.
- **Mirot's other estate-holders.** Decades of apothecary work means she knows who is unwell, who has been recently widowed, whose son drinks too much, whose daughter is sneaking out. She will share this freely with an investigating party — a murder investigation, in her view, justifies the relaxation of ordinary discretion. She will not gossip outside the investigation.
- **Halimon as a customer.** He buys eye-strain remedies and the occasional sleep aid. He has never bought anything that suggested forgery to her, and she has never asked. She has known him for years and has a careful neutral feeling about him. She finds him snide, which she does not hold against him.
- **She does not know about the loop.** Resets like everyone else.

If the shop is threatened she surrenders, hands open, and asks calmly what the visitor wants. She is not afraid of much; she has buried husbands, neighbors, and difficult customers, and her practical fearlessness about death is the kind earned by long exposure rather than naivety.

If a party were to threaten her for information, the conversation would go badly for them socially long before it went badly for her physically. She would tell them what they wanted to know — she has no reason not to — but they would have lost something they cannot easily get back. Mirot is a small town. People talk.

## DM Notes

**The "diluted poison" problem:** Rose's poison sale, on its own, is not enough to convict Darwinnith the Second. She sold the same compound to two households. The party needs to combine the poison with other evidence — Rose's witness testimony, Tovy Holm's account, the forged letter — to build a sufficient case. This is by design. The poison narrows the suspect pool to "someone with access to the Dundelver or Tahen households"; it does not point to one suspect.

**Rose as witness vector:** The dusk-walk testimony is one of the three key opportunity-fires (along with Tahen-Atep's unverified alibi and Tovy's eyewitness). It is also the most accessible — Rose is happy to talk and the party will likely engage her early as the village apothecary. A DM should be prepared to surface what she saw when the party asks general questions about Rezibund's recent movements or the evening of the murder. She will not volunteer the road encounter unless the conversation gets there; the party must ask about Rezibund's last day, or the evening, or unusual activity near the estate.

**Why she didn't ID the second figure:** She knows the Dundelvers well — she would recognize Darwinnith the Second on sight. The reason she cannot ID the man with Rezibund is structural: he sat with his back to her window throughout the dinner. She glanced over multiple times and saw only his back, the angle of one shoulder, the suggestion of dark hair. She did not see his face once. This is deliberate — if she had seen his face, the investigation would short-circuit. She is positioned as a partial witness whose testimony establishes a visitor existed but cannot identify them.

**Confirming the second figure as DD2:** The combination required is Rose's description (build, posture, dark hair) + a description of DD2 the party has gathered elsewhere (from Maret, Tovy, DD1, or direct observation). Rose will not name DD2 unprompted. With the matching description in hand, she will say it could have been him — and that the build is consistent. This is enough for the "opportunity" sub-step to fire on DD2.

**The wild shape potion commission (campaign history):** Already complete in the campaign timeline. The party brought her the Caliconia flower retrieved from the Misty Forest; she brewed 17 Potions of Wild Shape and split the yield with the party. The Caliconia flower has documented alchemical uses she is fully trained in, and the commission was the kind of journeyman-level work she has not had access to in over a decade. She lit up visibly when it was offered; she split the yield honestly; she would do it again in a heartbeat. This is established history — do not re-run the sequence. A future commission with a different rare reagent is open.

**Rose and the party:** If they treat her well, she returns the warmth — and her interest in the murder investigation makes her actively pleased to see them on follow-up visits. The wild shape commission was a high point of her professional decade. A returning party that re-establishes the relationship each cycle finds the same welcome each time. She does not remember them between cycles, but she is the kind of person who treats every first meeting like a warm one when the customer is interesting.

**As a returning resource:** Rose is a useful long-term ally for the party. Healing potions, herbal remedies, poison antidotes, alchemical commissions — she can supply or brew. She does not travel; her shop and garden are her life. But the party can return to her each cycle and get the same warm welcome and the same competent work.

**On reset:** Each cycle she begins fresh — no memory of the party, no awareness of the murder investigation, the standard reset state. A party that has already done the murder investigation in a previous cycle and returns to Rose for a different purpose will not get any recognition from her, which can be jarring if not anticipated. She remains useful; the relationship just doesn't accumulate.

**Joining hook:** None. She will not travel. Twenty years of practice are not portable on short notice, and she is not interested in leaving Mirot. She is a fixed resource — visit her, do not try to bring her along.
